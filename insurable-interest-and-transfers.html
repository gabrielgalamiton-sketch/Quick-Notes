PART I — GENERAL THEORY OF INSURABLE INTEREST AND INSURABLE INTEREST IN LIFE INSURANCE

1. Concept and Legal Definition of Insurable Interest

Imagine a stranger walking into an insurance brokerage and taking out a ₱10,000,000 fire policy over your family home or a ₱5,000,000 life policy over your neighbor. If your house burns down or your neighbor passes away, the stranger suffers zero financial grief—instead, he stands to collect a life-changing windfall.

Insurable interest is that relation between the insured and the event insured against such that the occurrence of the peril will cause substantial loss, damnification, or pecuniary harm to the insured, or the non-occurrence of which will preserve an existing legal or economic advantage. Cross-cutting Insurance Law, insurable interest is strictly pecuniary in nature—capable of monetary estimation—subject only to the classic exception of one's own life and close family ties.

THE PRACTICAL THRESHOLD TEST: "Anong Paki Mo?" (What is it to you?)
PERIL OCCURS → DIRECT FINANCIAL INJURY / LOSS = INSURABLE INTEREST EXISTS
PERIL OCCURS → NO PECUNIARY EFFECT WHATSOEVER = NO INSURABLE INTEREST (VOID WAGER)

Core requisites defining insurable interest across Philippine Insurance Law.

2. The Life Insurance Exception and the Nature of Policy Face Value

Imagine buying a ₱100,000,000 life insurance policy on yourself simply because you can afford the hefty annual premiums, even if your total net worth is only ₱2,000,000. When you pass away, the insurer cannot refuse to pay by arguing that your life was only worth ₱2,000,000 in economic value.

Human life is inherently beyond pecuniary valuation; hence, life insurance procured upon one's own life or the life of a spouse or child is the sole exception to the strictly pecuniary requirement of insurable interest. The face value of a life policy does not represent the monetary market worth of the human life; rather, it represents the maximum contractual ceiling of insurer liability, calibrated strictly against the policyholder's financial capacity to pay premiums.

⚠️ Examination Trap — Face Value as Valued Property vs. Life Ceiling: Never confuse a "valued property policy" with a life insurance policy. In property insurance, face value is subject to the strict principle of indemnity (actual cash value of the destroyed asset). In life insurance, human life cannot be over-insured or under-insured; the face value is an agreed contractual sum payable upon death, limited solely by the insured's ability to maintain premium payments.

3. Twin Legal Functions of Insurable Interest & The Ban on Wagering Policies

Imagine two coworkers betting ₱50,000 on whether the company CEO will survive major heart surgery next Monday. To give their gamble legal wrapping, one coworker attempts to buy a commercial life insurance policy on the CEO's life without the CEO's knowledge or support.

Insurable interest performs two indispensable functions: it serves as an essential requisite for contractual validity and acts as the determinative test of legal standing to insure. Under Section 25 of the Insurance Code and Article 2014 of the Civil Code, any purported insurance contract lacking insurable interest is degraded into an illegal wagering contract (pure gambling) and is void ab initio for violating public policy.

4. Insurable Interest in Life and Health: Family, Support, and Commercial Ties

Imagine a working son wanting to insure his retired 70-year-old father's life under Section 10(a), assuming that being a son automatically gives him insurable interest in his parent. Under Philippine law, that son is legally mistaken.

Under Section 10 of the Insurance Code, insurable interest in another person's life is strictly confined to four exclusive categories: Section 10(a) conclusively presumes insurable interest in oneself, spouse, and children based on blood and affection alone. Section 10(b) covers persons on whom the assured depends wholly or in part for education or support, as well as persons in whom the assured holds a direct pecuniary interest.

Memory Aid: S – D – C – U ("SAFE DUCS")
The four exclusive statutory categories of insurable interest in life and health (Sec. 10).
S (10[a])Self, Spouse, ChildrenConclusive presumption; natural affection and marriage bond suffice. No financial loss needed.
D (10[b])Dependency & PecuniaryRequires actual dependency for support/education (Art. 195 Family Code), or commercial/partner stake.
C (10[c])Creditor / ServicesDebtor owes money or employee performs crucial services. Capped strictly at debt/loss.
U (10[d])Usufruct / Vested InterestBeneficial estate or usufruct terminates upon the death of the life grantor.

Interplay Between Section 10(b) and Article 195 of the Family Code

While Article 195 of the Family Code establishes who is legally obliged to give support (spouses, ascendants, descendants, siblings), Section 10(b) imposes a mandatory qualification of actual dependency. An adult daughter cannot insure her wealthy father's life merely because he is legally obliged to support her; she must prove she actually depends wholly or in part upon him for education or living expenses.

DIRECTIONALITY OF DEPENDENCY: Only the DEPENDENT may insure the PROVIDER.
DEPENDENT → INSURES PROVIDER (Valid: provider's death inflicts pecuniary loss)
PROVIDER → INSURES DEPENDENT (Void under 10[b]: dependent's death causes grief, not financial injury)

⭐ Professor's Classroom Illustration: Vice Ganda & Ion Perez

Suppose celebrity Vice Ganda and her domestic partner Ion Perez—whose symbolic overseas marriage is not legally recognized under Philippine law—wish to procure life insurance on each other's lives:

  • Vice Ganda cannot insure Ion's life under Section 10(a): Ion is not a legal spouse under the Family Code, nor is he Vice's child.
  • Vice Ganda cannot insure Ion's life under Section 10(b): Vice does not depend on Ion for support or education. Ion's death would inflict emotional heartbreak, not pecuniary loss.
  • Ion CAN insure Vice Ganda's life under Section 10(b): If Vice Ganda is funding Ion's college tuition, business ventures, or monthly maintenance, Ion actually depends wholly or in part on Vice for support/education. Ion may validly buy a policy on Vice's life!
Statutory directionality under Section 10(b) vs. Beneficiary designation on one's own life under Section 11.
⚠️ Examination Trap — "Can a Son Insure His Father's Life Under Section 10(a)?": Bar examiners love this trap! Section 10(a) applies ONLY to oneself, spouse, and children. Parents and ascendants are strictly EXCLUDED from Section 10(a). A child can only insure a parent's life under Section 10(b) if the child actually depends on the parent for support or education. If the child is self-supporting, the policy is void!

5. Creditor-Debtor and Estate-Dependent Life Insurance (Sec. 10(c)–(d))

Imagine loaning ₱1,000,000 to an entrepreneur whose sudden death might leave his business bankrupt and your loan uncollectible. To secure your funds, you take out a ₱5,000,000 life insurance policy on his head.

Under Section 10(c), a creditor has an insurable interest in the life of a debtor, and an employer has an insurable interest in the life of a key employee, because death delays or prevents legal performance. Under Section 10(d), a person possessing an estate or vested interest (such as a usufructuary) has an insurable interest in the life of the person upon whose existence that estate depends.

Ceiling on Creditor Recovery & Functional Reclassification:
  • Strict Debt Ceiling: A creditor's insurable interest is strictly limited to the amount of the debt plus interest and premiums advanced. A ₱1,000,000 debt cannot support a ₱5,000,000 policy—the excess ₱4,000,000 is void as an illegal wager creating moral hazard.
  • Functional Property Reclassification: Creditor-debtor and employer-employee policies are doctrinally classified as property insurance (contracts of indemnity). The creditor is not insuring human life for sentimental reasons; he is protecting a credit receivable (personal property).
  • Application of the Two-Point Temporal Rule: Because it is treated as property insurance, insurable interest must exist both at perfection AND at the time of loss (death) under Section 19!

6. Procurement of Life Insurance on Another vs. Designation of Beneficiaries

Imagine naming a total stranger, your favorite Hollywood actor, or your non-marital romantic partner as the sole beneficiary of your ₱20,000,000 personal life insurance policy. When you pass away, can your estranged siblings invalidate the designation by claiming the beneficiary had zero insurable interest in you?

There is an absolute legal divide between procuring a policy over another's life (where the applicant must possess statutory insurable interest under Sec. 10) and designating a beneficiary in a policy on one's own life (governed by Civil Code rules on simple donations, where no insurable interest is required under Sec. 11 and Art. 2012).

PART II — TEMPORAL RULES IN LIFE INSURANCE AND INSURABLE INTEREST IN PROPERTY INSURANCE

7. Temporal Requirement in Life Insurance & The Creditor-Debtor Extinguishment Problem

Imagine taking out a life insurance policy on your spouse during your first year of marriage. Ten years later, your marriage ends in a bitter annulment and you never speak again. Five years after the annulment, your ex-spouse tragically passes away.

Under Section 19 of the Insurance Code, insurable interest in life and health must exist when the insurance takes effect, but need not exist thereafter or when the loss occurs. This is the Single-Point Rule: subsequent divorce, legal separation, or emancipation does not invalidate the policy.

"In pure life insurance, insurable interest is tested at birth, never at death."

The Two-Point Exception for Property-Nature Life Policies

The single-point rule does not apply to creditor-debtor [Sec. 10(c)] or employer-employee policies [Sec. 10(b)/(c)]. Because these are functionally property indemnity contracts, they follow the two-point temporal rule of property insurance: interest must exist both at effectivity and at the time of loss!

⭐ Professor's Classroom Illustration: Bea Alonzo & Angel Locsin (Extinguished Debt)

In January 2026, Angel Locsin (debtor / cestui que vie) borrowed ₱500,000,000 from Bea Alonzo (creditor / assured), payable in December 2026. Bea validly insured Angel's life for ₱500,000,000 under Section 10(c). In June 2026, Angel paid the ₱500,000,000 debt in full. In August 2026, Angel died.

  • Can Bea Alonzo recover the ₱500,000,000? NO. Creditor-debtor life insurance requires insurable interest to exist at two points: effectivity AND loss. When Angel fully paid in June, Bea's insurable interest was extinguished prior to Angel's death in August. Paying Bea would constitute unjust enrichment and reward moral hazard.
  • Can Angel's heirs/estate recover the ₱500,000,000? NO. Angel's heirs were neither parties to Bea's insurance contract nor designated beneficiaries.
  • Result: Neither party recovers the policy proceeds!

8. Insurable Interest in Property Insurance: Direct Damnification & Existing Interest

Imagine you just signed a binding contract to purchase a vintage sports car. You paid a 20% down payment, but the seller retains physical possession for detailing before final delivery. If a fire razes the detailing garage overnight, do you have legal standing to collect insurance proceeds?

Under Section 13 of the Insurance Code, insurable interest in property is measured by the Direct Damnification Test: every interest, relation, or liability of such nature that a contemplated peril might directly damnify the insured constitutes insurable interest. Under Section 14, an insurable interest in property may consist in: (a) an existing interest; (b) an inchoate interest founded on an existing interest; or (c) an expectancy coupled with an existing interest. The common and indispensable denominator across all three is an existing interest.

Section 14(a) Existing Interest: Legal Title vs. Equitable Title
HOLDERS OF LEGAL TITLE:
  • Absolute Owner: Registered titleholder in possession.
  • Unpaid Seller Prior to Delivery: Retains legal title as trustee for buyer.
  • Mortgagor: Retains legal title; mortgage is a mere encumbrance.
  • Lessor: Holds legal title while leasing possession (jus utendi).
HOLDERS OF EQUITABLE TITLE:
  • Buyer Prior to Delivery: Holds beneficial equitable interest in object.
  • Mortgagee: Bank/creditor holding security interest in collateral.
  • Mortgagor During Redemption: Retains equitable right to repurchase within statutory period after auction sale.

9. Modalities of Property Interest: Inchoate Rights & Coupled Expectancies

Imagine owning 30% of the shares of a family agro-industrial corporation. Can you buy a fire insurance policy in your personal name covering the corporation's warehouse and grain elevators, even though the corporation is a distinct juridical person?

Under Section 14(b), an inchoate interest (an unripened, prospective right) is insurable provided it is founded upon an existing interest. Under Section 14(c) in relation to Section 16, a mere hope or expectancy cannot be insured; an expectancy is insurable only when coupled with an existing interest in that out of which the expectancy arises.

Memory Aid: E – I – E
The three statutory modalities of insurable interest in property (Section 14).
E (14[a])Existing InterestPresent legal or equitable title (Owner, buyer before delivery, mortgagor, mortgagee, lessor).
I (14[b])Inchoate InterestIncomplete stake founded on a present legal relation: Stockholder insuring corporate property (stock ownership); Partner insuring partnership assets.
E (14[c])Coupled ExpectancyFuture profits/fruits tied to a present asset: Farmer/lessee insuring future crops; Breeder insuring unborn livestock; Shipowner insuring expected freightage; Store owner insuring against business interruption.
⚠️ Examination Trap — "Can an Heir Insure Property Expected from Living Parents?": Under Section 16, a mere contingent hope or bare expectancy is NOT insurable. An expectant heir has no existing interest in the properties of a living parent prior to death (succession opens only upon death under Civil Code Art. 777). A policy procured by an heir on a living parent's ancestral home is completely VOID!

10. Measure of Interest, Beneficiary Rules & The "Meantime Gap" Doctrine

Imagine you insure your car for ₱1,000,000, sell it to a colleague, and suffer immediate buyer's remorse. A month later, you repurchase the exact same car. Two weeks after repurchasing, a drunk driver totals the vehicle. Does your old policy cover the wreck?

Under Section 17, property insurance is strictly a contract of indemnity; the measure of insurable interest is the extent to which the insured might be damnified. Under Section 18, a designated beneficiary in property insurance must possess insurable interest in the property. Under Section 19, insurable interest in property must exist when the insurance takes effect, AND when the loss occurs, but need not exist in the meantime. This is the famous "Meantime Gap" Doctrine.

The "Meantime Gap" Doctrine (Sec. 19): Ownership gap merely suspends; repurchase before loss revives coverage.

⭐ Professor's Classroom Illustrations: Car Sale vs. Separation Anxiety

Hypo 1 — Car Sold Without Policy Assignment: A insures his car for ₱1,000,000 (Point 1 met). A sells and delivers the car to B without assigning the policy. While B is driving, the car is wrecked in an accident (Point 2). Result: Neither A nor B can recover! B cannot recover because B had no insurable interest at effectivity (Point 1). A cannot recover because A had no insurable interest at loss (Point 2).

Hypo 2 — The "Separation Anxiety" Repurchase: Same facts, but A repurchases the car from B one month later. While A owns the car again, an accident destroys it. Result: A can validly recover the full ₱1,000,000! Under Section 19, interest need not exist in the meantime. The interim sale merely suspended coverage under Section 20; once title reunited in A prior to loss, coverage fully revived.

11. Co-Existing Mortgage Interests & Special Mortgage Clauses

Imagine buying a ₱10,000,000 house with a ₱6,000,000 bank loan secured by a real estate mortgage. Both you and the lending bank want to protect your financial stakes against fire. Can two separate fire insurance policies co-exist on the exact same structure?

Under Section 8 of the Insurance Code, distinct insurable interests may co-exist simultaneously in the same property. In mortgaged property, the mortgagor has an insurable interest up to the full actual value of the property, while the mortgagee has a separate insurable interest limited strictly to the amount of the debt secured.

PART III — TRANSFER OF THING INSURED, POLICY, OR CLAIM AND STATUTORY EXCEPTIONS TO SUSPENSION

12. The Triad of Transfers in Insurance Law: Thing Insured, Policy, and Claim

Imagine your commercial warehouse is damaged by fire. The insurer agrees the payable claim is ₱15,000,000, but is taking weeks to release the check. Needing urgent working capital, can you sell your approved insurance claim to a bank or factoring firm without the insurer's permission?

In analyzing transfers in Insurance Law, three distinct objects must be kept strictly separate: (1) The thing insured (physical asset); (2) The insurance policy (written contract); and (3) The insurance claim (vested post-loss debt). Under Section 85, an agreement prohibiting the transfer of an insurance claim after loss has occurred is void if made before loss.

Comparative Analysis: The Triad of Transfers
Subject of Transfer Insurer Consent Required? Transferee Needs Insurable Interest? Legal Effect of Transfer
1. Life Insurance Policy (Secs. 184–185) NO (unless expressly required by policy stipulation). NO (transferee need not have insurable interest). Valid and fully enforceable by transferee (treated as an investment / chose in action).
2. Property / Casualty Policy (Secs. 20, 58) YES, strictly mandatory. Personal contract (delectus personae). YES (transferee must hold interest in property). Unauthorized transfer suspends policy until reunited (or voids if Sec. 25 clause exists).
3. The Thing Insured (Physical Asset) NO (insurance does not restrain owner's free alienation). N/A (buyer acquires property title by civil sale). Sale of property is valid, but suspends unassigned insurance under Section 20!
4. Vested Claim After Loss (Sec. 85) NO (anti-assignment stipulations are void). NO (claim is an accrued money debt / chose in action). Valid assignment; assignee may sue and collect proceeds directly.

13. Change of Interest in the Thing Insured: The General Rule of Suspension

Imagine you own a commercial building insured for ₱50,000,000. You sign a 5-year lease with a supermarket tenant and take out a ₱20,000,000 mortgage with a bank. Does entering into this lease or mortgage suspend your fire insurance policy?

Under Section 20 of the Insurance Code, a change of interest in any part of a thing insured, unaccompanied by a corresponding change of interest in the insurance, suspends the insurance to an equivalent extent, until the interest in the thing and the interest in the insurance are vested in the same person. Critically, Section 20 applies only to absolute transfers of ownership (sale, donation, exchange, dacion en pago).

SCOPE OF "CHANGE OF INTEREST" UNDER SECTION 20:
ABSOLUTE ALIENATION (Sale / Donation / Dacion) → SUSPENDS UNASSIGNED POLICY
MERE SECURITY / USE (Mortgage / Lease / Pledge) → DOES NOT SUSPEND (OWNERSHIP RETAINED)

14. Statutory Exceptions to the Section 20 Suspension Rule (Part 1)

Imagine you own four identical townhouses on a single street corner. You buy a single insurance policy that separately apportions ₱40,000,000 of coverage to each townhouse. If you sell Townhouse 1 without assigning the policy, and a fire subsequently burns all four townhouses to the ground, is your entire insurance suspended?

The Insurance Code carves out specific statutory exceptions where an unaccompanied transfer does not suspend the entire policy: Exception 1: Section 20 has zero application to life, health, or accident insurance. Exception 2 (Section 21): A change of interest after loss does not affect the insured's right to indemnity. Exception 3 (Section 22): A change of interest in one of several distinct things, separately insured by one policy, does not avoid or suspend insurance as to the others.

⭐ Professor's Classroom Illustration: The Four Adjacent Townhouses (Sec. 22 vs. Sec. 20)

A owns four adjacent townhouses (Houses 1, 2, 3, and 4) covered under a single policy, where each townhouse is separately insured for ₱40,000,000 (totaling ₱160,000,000). A sells House 1 to B without assigning the policy. A massive fire destroys all four townhouses:

  • Houses 2, 3, and 4: A validly recovers ₱120,000,000! Under Section 22, the contract is divisible. Selling House 1 has zero effect on the separate coverage of Houses 2, 3, and 4.
  • House 1: NEITHER A nor B can recover the ₱40,000,000! Coverage for House 1 was suspended under Section 20. A had no interest at loss; B had no interest at inception.

⭐ Professor's Classroom Illustration: Sale of Burned House After Loss (Section 21)

A insures his house for ₱20,000,000. While A is the owner, a fire razes the house to ashes. After the fire, A sells the burned lot and rubble to B without transferring the policy. Result: A is entitled to collect the entire ₱20,000,000 proceeds! Under Section 21 in relation to Section 19, both temporal points were fully met in A's person before the sale. Once loss occurs, A's right to indemnity is a vested property right; selling the debris does not trigger Section 20 suspension.

15. Statutory Exceptions to the Section 20 Suspension Rule (Part 2)

Imagine four law partners jointly buying and insuring an office building. One partner needs cash to study abroad and sells her 25% share to another existing partner. If the policy contains a clause stating "alienation voids this policy," does this internal partner buyout void the insurance?

The remaining statutory exceptions protect involuntary transfers, internal co-owner buyouts, and commercial open policies: Exception 4 (Section 23): Transfer by will or succession passes insurance automatically to the heir taking the property. Exception 5 (Section 24): Transfer among partners, joint owners, or co-owners who are jointly insured does not avoid or suspend the policy, even if an anti-alienation clause exists! Exception 6 (Section 25): An express prohibition against alienation voids/forfeits the policy upon sale to a stranger. Exception 7 (Section 57): Policies framed to inure to the benefit of whomsoever may become the owner ("for whom it may concern") automatically follow title.

⭐ Professor's Classroom Illustration: Buyout of Co-Owner's Share (Section 24)

Macalino, Juan, De Lera, and Paragas are co-owners of a residential house jointly insured under a single policy for ₱4,000,000. Needing funds for an upcoming trip abroad, Juan sells her undivided 25% share to fellow co-owner Macalino without insurer consent. Shortly thereafter, the house burns down:

  • Result: The three remaining co-owners (Macalino, De Lera, and Paragas) recover the full ₱4,000,000 insurance proceeds!
  • Doctrinal Rationale: Under Section 24, transfers among jointly insured co-owners do not suspend or avoid coverage. The insurer already vetted all four at contract inception—no outside stranger was introduced into the risk pool. Macalino's share simply grew, and all claimants satisfied both temporal points of Section 19!

Mnemonic: L – A – D – S – C – V – W ("LADS COW")
Statutory Provision Factual Trigger Operative Legal Effect
1. Life, Health & Accident (Sec. 20) Human life or bodily integrity. No suspension. Applies only to property; life requires interest only at inception (Sec. 19).
2. Transfer After Loss (Sec. 21) Sale of damaged property after fire/peril occurs. No suspension. Original insured recovers; indemnity already matured into a vested money claim.
3. Distinct Things Separately Insured (Sec. 22) Single policy separately values distinct properties (e.g. 4 townhouses). Divisible effect: Sold unit suspended under Sec. 20; unsold units remain completely covered under Sec. 22.
4. Will or Succession (Sec. 23) Insured owner dies; title transmits to heirs/devisees. No suspension. Policy passes automatically by operation of law (Civil Code Art. 777).
5. Joint Co-Owners / Partners (Sec. 24) One co-owner sells undivided share to another existing co-owner. No suspension or avoidance (even with anti-alienation clause); no stranger enters risk pool.
6. Express Anti-Alienation Clause (Sec. 25) Policy expressly states unauthorized alienation renders policy void. Policy is permanently avoided (forfeited) rather than merely suspended upon sale to an outsider.
7. "Whomsoever May Become Owner" (Sec. 57) Policy framed to inure to future owners ("for whom it may concern"). No suspension. Insurer gave advance blanket consent; coverage automatically follows ownership!

Top 5 Rules to Prioritize for Examination

  1. Directional Dependency Trap (Sec. 10[b]): Remember that dependency runs one way. The child/dependent can insure the supporting parent, but the parent cannot invoke dependency to insure the child (and must rely on blood/affection under Sec. 10[a]).
  2. Creditor-Debtor Extinguishment (Bea-Angel Hypo): Although codified under Section 10, creditor-debtor life insurance is functionally property insurance. If the loan is paid before the debtor dies, Point 2 of Section 19 fails, and the creditor recovers zero!
  3. Procuring on Another vs. Designating Beneficiary: If you buy insurance on someone else's life, you need Section 10 insurable interest. If you buy insurance on your own life, you can designate anyone as beneficiary (even friends or same-sex partners), subject only to Article 739 disqualifications.
  4. The "Meantime Gap" Repurchase: Selling an insured car without assigning the policy merely suspends coverage. If the original insured repurchases the vehicle before the accident, coverage automatically revives because both Point 1 and Point 2 are satisfied in the same person.
  5. Divisible Townhouses vs. Joint Co-Owners (Secs. 22 & 24): In divisible policies with separate valuations, selling Unit 1 leaves Units 2–4 intact. In internal co-owner buyouts, selling to an existing co-owner keeps coverage alive even if the contract contains a strict anti-alienation clause!
