PART I — General Concepts, Definitions & Charges (Arts. 924–928)

1. Concept, Definitions & Subject Matter (Article 924)

Imagine... Don Rafael writes a will leaving his vintage Rolex watch to his loyal driver and a specific 500-square-meter residential lot in Tagaytay to his favorite niece. However, before the executor pays the estate taxes and bank creditors, the driver and niece show up demanding immediate delivery of the watch and land title.

Under Article 924, all things and rights within the commerce of man may be bequeathed as a legacy (testamentary gift of personal property to a legatee) or devised as a devise (testamentary gift of real property to a devisee). Legatees and devisees succeed by particular title to individual, determinate items or specific rights—charged strictly against the disposable free portion—and under Pastor, Jr. v. CA, delivery cannot be enforced until the estate is fully liquidated.

Manresa's 4 Purposes of Bequests: (1) Moral/social duties, (2) Rewarding affection/devotion, (3) Gratitude for past favors/services, (4) Charitable/educational/religious funds.

2. Persons Charged & Extent of Liability (Articles 925 & 926)

Imagine... A testator leaves a net estate of ₱10,000,000 to his two legitimate children, Ana and Ben. Their combined legitime is ₱5,000,000 (₱2.5M each), and the testator gives Ana ₱4,000,000 of the free portion and Ben ₱1,000,000 of the free portion, while ordering "my children to pay a sub-legacy of ₱500,000 to my nurse."

Under Articles 925 and 926, a testator may charge compulsory heirs, voluntary heirs, or even legatees/devisees (a sub-legacy or sub-devise) with the payment of a bequest; if no one is specified, the estate itself bears the charge. A legatee/devisee is liable only up to the value of the gift received, while compulsory heirs are liable strictly in proportion to their share in the disposable free portion, because their legitime is completely untouchable.

3. Solidary Liability & Warranty Against Eviction (Articles 927 & 928)

Imagine... Three brothers take physical possession of their late father's mansion before turning over a bequeathed painting to a museum. One brother carelessly leaves a window open during a typhoon, destroying the painting; meanwhile, another heir delivers a generic stallion to a legatee, only for the true owner to evict the legatee a week later.

Under Article 927, if two or more heirs take possession of the estate, they are solidarily liable for the loss or destruction of a thing devised or bequeathed through negligence, fraud, or delay, even if only one co-heir was at fault. Under Article 928, the heir or estate is liable for eviction and hidden defects only when the bequest is generic/indeterminate, not when it is a specific thing chosen by the testator.

Symmetric Contrast Aphorisms (Arts. 927 & 928)
Rule / Provision Triggering Facts Legal Consequence
Art. 927: Solidary Liability of Co-Heirs
(Debuque v. Climaco)		 2+ heirs take physical possession of estate assets + bequeathed item is lost/destroyed via negligence (culpa), fraud (dolo), or delay (mora) of one heir. All co-heirs in possession are solidarily liable to the legatee/devisee (with right of reimbursement against the guilty co-heir). Also applies to unauthorized appropriation of estate funds.
Art. 928: Generic / Indeterminate Bequest Heir delivers an indeterminate item ("a horse") and legatee is evicted or discovers hidden defects. LIABLE for eviction (Art. 1548) and hidden defects because the obligor had the choice and duty to select an unencumbered item.
Art. 928: Specific / Determinate Bequest Testator bequeathed a specific item ("my horse Silver") and legatee is evicted by a third party. NOT liable for eviction because the testator specified the exact object, leaving the heir with no choice.

PART II — Property Owned Partly or Totally by Others or Legatees (Arts. 929–933)

4. Thing Belonging Partly to Testator or Stranger (Article 929)

Imagine... Testator T co-owns a beachfront parcel in Boracay equally (50–50) with his business partner S. In his will, T writes: "I devise the beachfront land in Boracay to my friend Marco."

Under Article 929, if the testator, heir, or legatee owns only a part of or an interest in the thing bequeathed, the legacy or devise is presumed limited strictly to such part or interest, unless the testator expressly declares that he gives the thing in its entirety (knowing that he is only a part-owner).

Arrow Micro-Formula: Partial Ownership Presumption (Art. 929)

PARTIAL OWNERSHIP → PRESUMED LIMITED TO TESTATOR'S SHARE → ENTIRETY ONLY IF EXPRESSLY DECLARED + KNOWLEDGE

Partition: Adjudicated to Testator
Bequest Subsists in Full
If before death the property is partitioned and awarded to the testator, the devisee gets it.
Partition: Adjudicated to Co-Owner
Inoperative (Art. 957[2])
Without an express declaration of entirety, adjudication to the co-owner works as an implied alienation.

5. Thing Belonging Entirely to a Stranger (Articles 930 & 931)

Imagine... Testator T bequeaths his neighbor's painting to L. In Scenario A, T genuinely thought he owned the painting because it had been hanging in his living room for years. In Scenario B, T knew it belonged to his neighbor and directed his executor to buy it for L, but the neighbor demands ₱50 Million for a ₱500,000 painting.

Under Article 930, a bequest of a stranger's property is void if the testator erroneously believed he owned it—unless the testator subsequently acquires it before death by any title. Under Article 931, if the testator knew it belonged to another and ordered its acquisition, the bequest is valid: the estate must buy and deliver it, or if the owner refuses to sell or demands an excessive price, pay the legatee its just value.

Juridical Debate on Art. 931: Justices J.B.L. Reyes, Puno & Caguioa require an express order to acquire; Tolentino, Jurado & Paras hold that knowledge alone creates an implied order to acquire.

6. Thing Already Belonging to Legatee or Devisee (Articles 932 & 933)

Imagine... Testator T bequeaths a specific condo unit to Carla. What if Carla already owned that condo on the day T signed the will? Or what if a stranger owned it when T signed the will, and Carla bought it for ₱5 Million a year before T died?

Under Articles 932 and 933, if the thing already belonged to the legatee/devisee at the execution of the will, the bequest is ineffective/void (even if the legatee later alienates it), unless the testator expressly orders it freed from a third-party encumbrance. However, if the legatee acquires it after the execution of the will (assuming it was a valid bequest under Art. 931), a gratuitous acquisition yields nothing, whereas an onerous acquisition entitles the legatee to reimbursement from the estate.

Symmetric Contrast: WHEN & HOW the Legatee Acquired It (Arts. 932–933)
Timing & Mode of Legatee's Ownership Legal Effect / Remedy
1. Owned by Legatee AT Execution of Will (Arts. 932 & 933 par. 1) VOID / INEFFECTIVE, even if legatee later sells it.
Exception: Valid to the extent the testator expressly orders the property freed from an encumbrance (mortgage, usufruct, pledge).
2. Acquired AFTER Will Execution by GRATUITOUS Title (Art. 933 par. 2) Claims Nothing. Because the legatee already got the item for free (by donation or inheritance), the testator's liberality is fulfilled.
3. Acquired AFTER Will Execution by ONEROUS Title from Stranger (Art. 933 par. 2) Demands Reimbursement from the estate/heir for the purchase price or value paid (assuming the bequest was valid under Art. 931).
4. Acquired AFTER Will Execution by Purchase from TESTATOR Himself Revoked by Alienation (Art. 957[2]); No Reimbursement. By selling the item to the legatee, the testator impliedly revoked the gratuitous bequest.

PART III — Encumbered Property, Credits, Remission & Creditors (Arts. 934–939)

7. Pledged or Mortgaged Property & Other Charges (Article 934)

Imagine... Testator T devises a commercial building to his nephew. The building is both (a) mortgaged to BPI to secure a ₱10M loan, and (b) burdened by a 10-year registered usufruct and right-of-way easement in favor of a neighbor. Who pays the ₱10M bank loan, and does the estate have to buy out the usufruct?

Under Article 934, if a bequeathed thing is pledged or mortgaged to secure a recoverable debt (whether before or after the execution of the will), the estate is obliged to pay the debt unless a contrary intention appears. Conversely, any other charge (perpetual or temporary, such as usufructs, leases, or easements) passes with the property to the legatee or devisee.

Symmetric Contrast: Debt Security vs. Real Burden (Art. 934)
Pledge, Mortgage, Antichresis
Estate Pays & Clears Title

Applies whether constituted before or after the will, as long as it secures a recoverable debt (unless testator orders otherwise).

Usufruct, Servitude, Lease
Passes to Beneficiary

Perpetual or temporary non-debt charges follow the property; the devisee must respect them until extinguished.

Mortgagee's 3 Alternative Remedies in Probate (Rule 86, Sec. 7): (1) Abandon security & file an ordinary money claim against the estate; (2) Foreclose judicially against the executor/administrator and claim any deficiency in probate; or (3) Rely solely on the mortgage and foreclose extrajudicially/judicially within 10 years (waiving any deficiency).

8. Legacy of Credit & Remission of Debt (Articles 935–937)

Imagine... Testator T bequeaths to L "the ₱1,000,000 debt that Debtor D owes me." Six months before dying, T pays a lawyer to file a collection suit in RTC against D, though D still hasn't paid a cent when T dies. What happens to L's legacy?

Under Articles 935 to 937, a legacy of credit (against a third person) or legacy of remission (releasing the legatee's own debt) is effective only for the balance existing at the testator's death, and lapses by implied revocation if the testator brings a judicial action against the debtor for collection (even if unpaid at death).

Memory aid: C-R-A-P-G Rules on Credits & Remissions (Arts. 935–937)
CCredit Legacy (Art. 935)Effective only for the unpaid amount + interest existing at death. Executed by assigning the right of action to the legatee.
RRemission (Art. 935)Condonation of legatee's debt; effective only for the balance existing at death. Estate issues an acquittance.
AAction in Court (Art. 936)Filing a judicial action revokes the legacy even if unpaid at death. Mere extrajudicial demand does NOT revoke it!
PPledge Return (Art. 936)Bequeathing the pledged thing back to the debtor extinguishes only the accessory pledge; the principal debt remains due.
GGeneric Release (Art. 937)A generic release ("I forgive all debts L owes me") covers only debts existing at execution of the will, not subsequent ones.
⚠️ The Favorite Examination Trap (Judicial Suit vs. Demand Letter & Death vs. Execution Date): Watch out for two classic traps: (1) If the testator sent five angry notarized demand letters to the debtor before dying, the legacy of credit/remission is NOT revoked (unless the debtor actually paid, leaving zero balance at death). Only a judicial action revokes it under Art. 936! (2) A specific legacy of credit/remission is measured at death (Art. 935), whereas a generic remission of debts covers only debts existing at will execution (Art. 937).

9. Legacy to Creditors & Mistaken Debts (Articles 938 & 939)

Imagine... Testator T owes Creditor C ₱500,000. In his will, T writes: "I give C a legacy of ₱500,000." Can the executor refuse to pay C's debt claim by arguing that the ₱500,000 legacy already paid it off? What if T instead wrote: "Pay X the ₱300,000 I owe him," when T actually owed X nothing—or when the debt had already prescribed 12 years ago?

Under Article 938, a legacy or devise to a creditor is NOT applied to his credit unless the testator expressly declares it; thus, the creditor collects both his credit and his legacy. Under Article 939, an order to pay a non-existent debt is considered not written (void) and any excess over a true debt is not due, except when the testator orders the fulfillment of a natural obligation (such as a prescribed debt).

Testamentary Provision Legal Rule & Effect
Legacy to a Creditor (Art. 938) Presumed a pure act of liberality. Creditor collects BOTH the debt (against the gross estate) and the legacy (from the free portion), unless testator expressly declares it applies to the credit.
Order to Pay Non-Existent / Excess Debt (Art. 939) Void / Considered Not Written due to mistake (solutio indebiti); excess over real debt is not due unless intent to give a legacy is proved.
Order to Pay a Prescribed Debt (Art. 939 par. 2) VALID & ENFORCEABLE as the voluntary fulfillment of a natural obligation (Art. 1424).
Mere Listing of Debts in Will (Santos v. Manarang) Directing the executor to pay listed debts is NOT a legacy. Creditors must still file their money claims within the probate statute of non-claims or be forever barred.

PART IV — Alternative, Generic & Welfare Bequests (Arts. 940–945)

10. Alternative & Generic Legacies and Devises (Articles 940–943)

Imagine... Testator T dies leaving an estate consisting entirely of cash in the bank—zero cars and zero land. In his will, he gives L1 "a brand-new sedan" (generic personal property) and L2 "a one-hectare residential lot" (generic real property). One of these gifts is completely valid, and the other is dead on arrival!

Under Article 940, in an alternative bequest, the right of choice belongs to the executor/administrator or heir charged (unless expressly given to the legatee/devisee) and becomes irrevocable once communicated. Under Article 941, a generic legacy of personal property is valid even if none of its kind exists in the estate, whereas a generic devise of real property is valid ONLY if immovable property of its kind exists in the estate.

Symmetric Contrast: Generic Movable vs. Generic Immovable (Art. 941)
Generic PERSONAL Property
ALWAYS VALID

Valid even if there are no things of the same kind in the estate at death. The executor must acquire and deliver one of medium quality.

Generic REAL Property
VALID ONLY IF IN ESTATE

Valid only if immovable property of its kind exists in the estate (because land is artificially delineated by man). Check Art. 793 for after-acquired realty.

Right of Choice Rules (Arts. 940–943): (1) Default chooser = Executor/Heir charged, who must pick medium quality ("neither inferior nor superior"). (2) If expressly granted to the legatee/devisee, the beneficiary may pick the best quality available in the estate! (3) If the chooser dies before choosing, the right passes to his heirs; once made, the choice is irrevocable.

11. Legacies for Education, Support & Pensions (Articles 944 & 945)

Imagine... A wealthy godfather leaves three welfare legacies in his will: (1) a legacy for the education of his 16-year-old godson, (2) a legacy for the support of his retired househelp, and (3) a monthly pension of ₱30,000 payable at the start of every month to his cousin, who receives the January 1 payout and dies on January 5.

Under Articles 944 and 945, a legacy for education lasts until the age of majority (18) or beyond until the legatee finishes a professional, vocational, or general course (if pursued diligently); a legacy for support lasts during the legatee's lifetime; and a periodic pension is demandable upon the testator's death and at the start of each period, with no duty to refund even if the legatee dies before the period ends.

Welfare Bequest Duration Amount & Special Rules
Legacy for Education
(Art. 944 par. 1)		 Until majority (18) OR beyond to finish a professional, vocational, or general course (with diligence). If unfixed by testator, fixed according to the social standing and circumstances of the testator and the value of the disposable estate. Strictly personal.
Legacy for Support
(Art. 944 pars. 2–4)		 During the legatee's lifetime (unless testator specifies otherwise). If testator habitually gave support in life, that same amount applies unless markedly disproportionate to the disposable estate.
Legacy of Periodic Pension
(Art. 945)		 Periodic (weekly, monthly, yearly) as fixed in the will. First period demandable at testator's death; subsequent ones at the start of each period. Once paid/due, NEVER refunded even if legatee dies mid-period. (Subject to Rule 90, Sec. 1 bond/liquidation).

PART V — Vesting, Preference, Delivery & Implied Revocation (Arts. 946–959)

12. Vesting of Rights, Ownership, Fruits & Risks (Articles 946–949)

Imagine... Testator T leaves a pure and simple devise of a mango orchard to D, and a generic legacy of 100 sacks of rice to G. Between the day T dies and the day the executor delivers the properties six months later, the mango orchard harvests ₱200,000 worth of mangoes. Who owns those mangoes?

Under Articles 947 to 949, for a pure and simple bequest of a specific, determinate thing belonging to the testator, both the right and ownership vest at the moment of the testator's death, carrying with them all fruits and income (and risk of fortuitous loss under res perit domino) from death. For generic or conditional/term gifts, ownership and fruits vest only upon selection/choice or fulfillment/arrival.

Master Matrix: Vesting of Right, Ownership, Fruits & Risk (Arts. 947–949)
Type of Bequest Right Vests Ownership Vests Fruits & Income Risk of Loss
1. Pure & Simple (Specific) Moment of Death Moment of Death From Moment of Death (excludes rent/interest already due before death) Legatee/Devisee from death
2. With Suspensive Term Moment of Death (transmissible) Arrival of Term From Arrival of Term Estate until term arrives
3. Suspensive Condition Upon Fulfillment Fulfillment (retroacts to death) From Fulfillment (unless testator orders otherwise) Estate until fulfilled
4. Generic / Quantity Moment of Death Upon Selection / Choice From Selection / Choice (unless ordered from death) Genus nunquam perit
5. Money Legacy Moment of Death Upon Payment 6% p.a. interest from judicial demand / default (Chiong Joc-Soy) Estate
Note on Usufruct vs. Legacy (Art. 946): If the bequeathed thing was subject to a usufruct, the legatee/devisee must respect the usufruct until it is legally extinguished.

13. Order of Preference: Article 950 vs. Article 911

Imagine... A bachelor dies with no compulsory heirs and no donations inter vivos, leaving a net estate of ₱5,000,000. However, his will contains six different legacies totaling ₱10,000,000. Do we cut all six legacies by 50% across the board, or do some legacies get paid 100% first?

Under Article 950, when the estate (or disposable free portion) is insufficient to cover all legacies and devises and the conflict is exclusively among the legatees/devisees themselves, they are paid strictly in the 6-tier hierarchical order of R – P – S – E – S – A. Conversely, if reduction is needed to preserve the legitime of compulsory heirs or when donations inter vivos concur, Article 911 governs and reduces non-preferred legacies pro rata.

⚠️ The Favorite Examination Trap (When to Apply Art. 911 vs. Art. 950):
  • Use Article 911 (Pro Rata Reduction of Non-Preferred Legacies): Whenever (a) legitimes of compulsory heirs are impaired, OR (b) there are donations inter vivos concurring with legacies/devises. Under Art. 911, support, education, and specific gifts are all cut pro rata together before touching testator-preferred gifts!
  • Use Article 950 (R-P-S-E-S-A Ladder): ONLY when the conflict is exclusively among legatees/devisees (e.g., no compulsory heirs exist, or the legitimes are already untouched and there are no donations inter vivos, or when distributing among heirs who were charged beyond their free portion).

14. Delivery, Self-Possession, Acceptance & Merging (Articles 951–956)

Imagine... Testator T leaves two legacies to L: Legacy A is a free ₱1,000,000 diamond ring (purely gratuitous), and Legacy B is an apartment building worth ₱3,000,000 burdened with an obligation to house T's elderly sister for life (onerous). Can L grab the diamond ring from T's dresser on the night of the wake, accept Legacy A, and reject Legacy B?

Under Article 953, a legatee or devisee cannot take possession on his own authority, even if ownership vested at death; he must request delivery from the executor/administrator or heir. Under Articles 954 and 955, a beneficiary cannot accept a gratuitous legacy (or part) and repudiate an onerous one. Under Article 956, any ineffective or repudiated bequest merges into the mass of the estate (passing by intestacy if there is no substitution or accretion, as in Parish Priest of Victoria, Tarlac v. Rigor).

Symmetric Contrast: Acceptance & Repudiation Matrix (Arts. 954–955)
Combination of Bequests to Same Beneficiary Can Beneficiary Accept One & Reject the Other?
Single Legacy: Partly Gratuitous, Partly Onerous (Art. 954) NO. Cannot accept the free part and repudiate the burdened part. (If he dies before accepting, his heirs may accept their shares independently on the same terms).
Two Legacies: One Gratuitous, One Onerous (Art. 955 par. 1) NO as to rejecting the onerous while keeping the gratuitous. (He can accept the onerous and reject the gratuitous, or accept/reject both).
Two Legacies: Both Onerous OR Both Gratuitous (Art. 955 par. 1) YES. He may accept or renounce either or both, unless the testator expressly made them inseparable.
Compulsory Heir who is also a Legatee/Devisee (Art. 955 par. 2) YES. May waive the inheritance/legitime and accept the legacy, or waive the legacy and accept the legitime.
ISRAI Cascade when a Legacy Fails (Art. 956; Parish Priest of Victoria v. Rigor): If a legacy fails due to predecease, incapacity, repudiation, or unfulfilled condition, check: Substitution → Accretion → Intestacy (Merges into Mass of Estate). Delivery includes all accessions/accessories in the condition at death (Art. 951).

15. Revocation by Implication of Law (Article 957)

Imagine... Testator T bequeaths his gold bar to L1, his Tagaytay house to L2, and his racehorse to L3. Before dying, T melts the gold bar into a gold crown, sells the Tagaytay house to X (and buys it back two years later), and the racehorse is struck by lightning. Are any of these three legacies still alive?

Under Article 957, a legacy or devise is impliedly revoked by operation of law in three instances: (1) Transformation, (2) Alienation, and (3) Total Loss—memorized as T – A – L.

Memory aid: T – A – L (3 Grounds for Implied Revocation under Art. 957)
Letter Ground Requisites, Doctrines & Exceptions
T Transformation
(Art. 957[1])		 Testator transforms the thing so that it retains neither its form (external appearance) nor its denomination (name/identity)—e.g., wool turned into a suit, riceland converted into a commercial fishpond.
A Alienation
(Art. 957[2])		 Testator voluntarily alienates the thing (or part of it) by any title. General Rule: Even if the alienation is void or the testator reacquires the property, the legacy is NOT revived.
2 Exceptions (Legacy Survives!):
1. Reacquisition by exercising a right of repurchase (pacto de retro).
2. Alienation was annulled due to vitiated consent (fraud, violence, undue influence—Fernandez v. Dimagiba) or was an involuntary execution/tax sale, because the testator lacked voluntary revocatory intent.
L Loss of the Thing
(Art. 957[3])		 Thing is totally lost during the testator's lifetime, or after his death without the heir's fault (unless the heir/obligor is liable for eviction under Art. 928 for generic items).

16. Mistake in Description & Dispositions to Relatives (Articles 958 & 959)

Imagine... A childless testator leaves a will stating: "I leave all my remaining properties to my relatives." He is survived by an uncle (3rd degree), a nephew (3rd degree, whose brother predeceased leaving a grandnephew in the 4th degree), and a first cousin (4th degree). Does the grandnephew inherit by representing his predeceased father?

Under Article 958, a mistake in the name or description of a thing does not vitiate a bequest if the object can be ascertained. Under Article 959, a disposition made in general terms in favor of the testator's relatives applies strictly in favor of those nearest in degree (within the 5th collateral degree). Because Art. 959 only adopts the Rule of Proximity, there is NO right of representation, no preference of lines, and no 2:1 full/half-blood ratio!

Two Landmark Doctrines: (1) Belen v. BPI: Art. 959 applies only to "relatives of the testator," NOT "relatives of Legatee X." (2) Vda. de Singson v. De Lim: If the will says "to all who are entitled thereto," full intestate rules (including representation) apply!

1. T executed a will devising Lot X to D. Later, through fraud and undue influence exerted by M, T signed a deed of sale of Lot X to M. Before dying, T filed an action and obtained a final judgment annulling the sale for vitiated consent. Does D still get Lot X?
2. T's will states: "I bequeath ₱10 Million to my relatives." T is survived by his maternal Uncle U (3rd degree), his full-blood Nephew N1 (3rd degree), and his Grandnephew GN (4th degree, son of predeceased Nephew N2). How is the ₱10M divided?

Top 5 Rules to Prioritize for the Bar Examination

  1. Generic Movable vs. Generic Immovable (Art. 941): A legacy of generic personal property ("a car") is valid even if none exists in the estate; a devise of generic real property ("a residential lot") is void if no immovable of its kind exists in the estate.
  2. Implied Revocation by Alienation & Its Two Exceptions (Art. 957[2]): Voluntary alienation revokes a legacy forever even if the testator later buys the item back—unless (a) the reacquisition was by exercising a pacto de retro right of repurchase, or (b) the alienation was annulled for vitiated consent (Fernandez v. Dimagiba).
  3. Judicial Suit vs. Extrajudicial Demand in Legacy of Credit/Remission (Art. 936): Only a judicial action filed by the testator against the debtor revokes a legacy of credit or remission; demand letters do not.
  4. Art. 911 vs. Art. 950 Order of Preference: Memorize R-P-S-E-S-A for conflicts exclusively among legatees/devisees (Art. 950), but switch to Art. 911 whenever compulsory heirs' legitimes are impaired or donations inter vivos exist.
  5. Article 959 "Relatives" Rule: "To my relatives" means only the rule of proximity within the 5th degree—never apply the right of representation or the 2:1 full/half-blood rule!
