PART I — General Principles & The Descending Direct Line (Arts. 978–984)

1. Underlying Principles & Order of Intestate Succession (Art. 978)

Imagine a wealthy patriarch who dies suddenly without leaving a will. His grieving children, aged parents, widow, illegitimate child, and brothers all show up claiming a share of the estate.

Intestate succession operates on the presumed will of the decedent, governed by four bedrock principles: the direction of love ("love first descends, then ascends, and finally spreads sideways"), the rule of proximity (nearer degree excludes more distant, saving representation), the rule of equal division (with 3 statutory exceptions), and exclusion vs. concurrence.

Memory aid: DOWN → UP → SIDE (With 3 Exceptions to Equal Division)
1. DESCENDING DIRECT LINE (First Priority) Legitimate children & descendants exclude ascendants and all collaterals
↓
2. ASCENDING DIRECT LINE (Second Priority — In Default of Descendants) Legitimate parents & ascendants exclude all collaterals
↓
3. COLLATERAL LINE (Spreads Sideways up to 5th Degree) Inherits only in default of descendants, ascendants, illegitimate children, and spouse (except siblings/nephews/nieces who concur with spouse)
Three Statutory Exceptions to Equal Division in the Same Degree (Mnemonic: L – B – R):
1. Lines in the Ascending Line (50% Paternal line / 50% Maternal line — Art. 987)
2. Blood Relationship among Siblings/Nephews (2:1 Full-Blood vs. Half-Blood — Art. 1006)
3. Representation (Division per stirpes by family root — Art. 974)

2. Legitimate, Legitimated & Adopted Children (Arts. 979 & 984)

Imagine a man who has children from two different marriages and one legally adopted daughter. Upon his death, the children of the first marriage claim a larger share based on seniority, while the biological parents of the adopted child later claim her estate when she dies childless.

Under Article 979, legitimate and legitimated children inherit in equal shares without distinction as to sex, age, or different marriages, and an adopted child succeeds in the same manner as a legitimate child. Under R.A. 8552 (Sec. 18) (now reaffirmed by R.A. 11642, Sec. 41), Article 984 of the Civil Code has been modified so that adopters and adoptees are reciprocal legal and compulsory heirs, severing intestate successional ties with biological parents.

Evolution of Successional Rights in Adoption (Chronological Flow)
STAGE 1: Civil Code (Art. 343 & Art. 984) & PD 603 If adopter left legitimate parents/ascendants, adopted child was reduced to the share of an acknowledged natural child. Under Art. 984, if adoptee died without issue, biological parents inherited intestate.
↓
STAGE 2: Family Code (Art. 189) Expressly repealed Art. 343 NCC. Adopted child acquired full successional rights as a legitimate child of the adopter, while still remaining an intestate heir of biological parents under Art. 189(3).
↓
STAGE 3: Domestic Adoption Act (RA 8552, Sec. 18) & RA 11642 (Sec. 41) Severed all intestate ties with biological parents. Adopter(s) and adoptee have exclusive reciprocal intestate rights. Biological parents can inherit from the adoptee ONLY if named as voluntary beneficiaries in a valid will.
🪤 The Favorite Examination Trap — Bartolome v. SSS (740 SCRA 78) Reversion Exception:
Does the death of a sole adopting parent restore the biological parents' right to inherit from the adopted child? Read carefully when the adopter died!
• If the sole adopter dies during the MINORITY of the adopted child (Bartolome v. SSS; now R.A. 11642 Sec. 42): Parental authority and legal custody automatically revert to the biological parents (since adoption is a personal relationship and the minor child cannot be left without parental care). Consequently, the biological parent qualifies as a dependent parent/beneficiary upon the adoptee's subsequent death.
• If the adoptee is already emancipated/of age when the adopter dies (Regular Rule under RA 8552 Sec. 18): The adopter's estate passes to the adoptee, and the biological parents remain excluded from intestate succession to the adoptee's estate.

3. Per Capita vs. Per Stirpes in the Descending Line (Arts. 980–982)

Imagine a decedent leaving a ₱1.2 Million estate. He is survived by two living children (A and B), two grandchildren (X and Y) from predeceased child C, and three grandchildren (M, N, and O) from predeceased child D.

Surviving children in the first degree always inherit in their own right per capita (equal shares per head, Art. 980), whereas grandchildren stepping into the shoes of a predeceased, incapacitated, or disinherited child inherit by right of representation per stirpes (by family root, Arts. 981–982)—even if all children have died and only grandchildren survive (Art. 982).

Symmetric Contrast Aphorism: ALL CHILDREN ALIVE = PER CAPITA (Art. 980)  |  GRANDCHILDREN BY REPRESENTATION = PER STIRPES EVEN IF ALL CHILDREN DIED (Art. 982)
(Compare this with Nephews/Nieces in Art. 1005: if all siblings are dead, nephews/nieces inherit PER CAPITA, whereas grandchildren under Art. 982 still inherit PER STIRPES unless all children repudiated under Art. 969!)

4. Concurrence of Legitimate & Illegitimate Children (Art. 983)

Imagine a decedent who leaves one legitimate child and five illegitimate children, with an estate of ₱1,200,000. If you blindly give every illegitimate child half of a legitimate child's share, the lone legitimate child would get less than half of the estate!

Under Article 983 in relation to Article 176 of the Family Code, each illegitimate child receives one-half (1/2) of the share of each legitimate child (2:1 ratio), provided that the collective shares of the illegitimate children must never impair the 1/2 compulsory legitime of the legitimate children.

Step 2 (Check Impairment): Legitimate children get ₱800k total (2/3 of estate), which is ≥ ₱600k (1/2 legitime). No impairment!

PART II — Ascending Direct Line & Illegitimate Children (Arts. 985–994)

5. Legitimate Parents & Ascendants: Division by Lines (Arts. 985–987)

Imagine an unmarried professional who dies childless leaving ₱1,000,000. Both of his parents are dead, leaving only his paternal grandfather (A) and his maternal grandparents (C and D).

In default of legitimate descendants, legitimate parents and ascendants inherit to the exclusion of all collateral relatives (Art. 985). There is never any right of representation in the ascending line: if one parent survives, that parent takes 100% of the ascendants' share excluding the grandparents on the deceased parent's side (Art. 986); if both parents are dead and ascendants of equal degree belong to different lines, the estate is divided 50% to the paternal line and 50% to the maternal line, and then per capita within each line (Art. 987).

Two-Step Ascending Formula: NEAREST DEGREE EXCLUDES REMOTE → SPLIT 50/50 BY LINE → DIVIDE PER CAPITA INSIDE EACH LINE

6. Illegitimate Children & Their Descendants (Arts. 988–991)

Imagine a decedent who has no legitimate children, leaving only his legitimate parents and three illegitimate children (one of whom predeceased him, leaving a legitimate child and an illegitimate child).

In the absence of legitimate descendants and ascendants, illegitimate children inherit the entire estate to the exclusion of collateral relatives (Art. 988). Unlike a legitimate child (under the classic Art. 992 view), an illegitimate child can be represented by BOTH legitimate and illegitimate descendants (Arts. 989–990, observing the 2:1 ratio between them). When illegitimate children concur with legitimate ascendants (Art. 991), they split the estate 1/2 to legitimate ascendants and 1/2 to illegitimate children regardless of headcount.

🪤 The Favorite Examination Trap — Justice J.B.L. Reyes' Rule in Partial Intestacy (Art. 991):
Suppose X dies leaving Legitimate Parents (LP) and Illegitimate Children (IC) with an estate of ₱1,000,000, and his will merely gives a ₱100,000 legacy to a friend. How do you distribute the estate?
• Wrong Answer: Deduct ₱100,000 off the top and split ₱900,000 (giving LP ₱450k and IC ₱450k). That illegally impairs LP's compulsory legitime of 1/2 (₱500,000)!
• Correct Answer (J.B.L. Reyes' Rule): Deduct testamentary legacies exclusively from the 1/2 share of the illegitimate children so that the 1/2 legitime of the legitimate ascendants remains completely untouched (LP = ₱500,000; Friend = ₱100,000; IC = ₱400,000).

7. The Barrier ("Iron Curtain") Rule & Jurisprudence (Art. 992)

Imagine an illegitimate son who grows up loving his father's legitimate parents. When the father dies ahead of the grandfather, and the grandfather later dies intestate, the legitimate uncles tell the illegitimate grandson: "You cannot inherit a single centavo from our father by law!"

Article 992 erects a statutory barrier (the "Iron Curtain") providing that an illegitimate child has no right to inherit ab intestato from the legitimate children and relatives of his father or mother, nor shall such legitimate children or relatives inherit in the same manner from the illegitimate child.

Visualizing the "Iron Curtain" (Art. 992) vs. Representation Rules
*Note: Under Aquino v. Aquino (2021), the Supreme Court abandoned Diaz v. IAC in Case 1 and now allows an illegitimate grandchild to represent a legitimate parent in the grandparent's estate.
Memory aid: 5 Key Jurisprudential Rules on Art. 992 (D – V – W – I – S)
D Diaz v. IAC (150 SCRA 645) & Llorente v. Rodriguez Classic lecture rule: An illegitimate child cannot represent his deceased legitimate parent in the estate of the legitimate grandparent based on presumed antagonism (Grey v. Fabie). (Abandoned in 2021 by Aquino v. Aquino as to grandchildren).
V Vda. de Crisologo v. CA (137 SCRA 231) The barrier is reciprocal: the legitimate mother's legitimate relatives (aunts/uncles/cousins) cannot inherit ab intestato from the mother's illegitimate child.
W Wills & Voluntary Dispositions Exempt Article 992 restricts only succession by operation of law (ab intestato and legitimes). It does not prohibit voluntary testamentary bequests or fideicommissary substitutions.
I Delgado Vda. de Dela Rosa v. Heirs of Damian (480 SCRA 334); Manuel v. Ferrer The barrier does not apply between illegitimate siblings! Full-blood or half-blood illegitimate brothers and sisters inherit intestate from one another (observing the 2:1 full-to-half blood ratio).
S Suntay III v. Cojuangco-Suntay (621 SCRA 142 & 683 SCRA 449) While the 2010 decision suggested love and joint upbringing could rebut the presumption of antagonism, the 2012 Resolution clarified that Art. 992 remains an absolute statutory rule in declaring heirship (though inapplicable when appointing a co-administrator).

8. Succession to the Estate of an Illegitimate Child (Arts. 993–994)

Imagine an illegitimate child who becomes a self-made millionaire and dies intestate without any children. His biological father, his biological grandfather, his widow, his legitimate half-brother, and his illegitimate brother all claim a share.

When an illegitimate child dies without issue (neither legitimate nor illegitimate descendants), his estate passes first to his illegitimate parents (excluding grandparents and higher ascendants, Art. 993). In default of parents, the surviving spouse takes the entire estate, or splits it 1/2 to the spouse and 1/2 to illegitimate brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces (Art. 994, excluding legitimate siblings under Art. 992).

4 Core Rules When the Decedent Is an Illegitimate Child (Arts. 993–994)
1. PARENTS STOP AT DEGREE 1 (Art. 993)

Only the father or mother (whose filiation is duly proved) inherits. Illegitimate grandparents and higher ascendants have zero intestate rights.

2. ILLEGITIMATE PARENTS + SPOUSE (Analogy to Art. 997)

Though omitted in the text of Arts. 993–994, Justice J.B.L. Reyes & the Code Commission confirm that they concur: 1/2 to Illegitimate Parents, 1/2 to Surviving Spouse.

3. SPOUSE + ILLEGITIMATE SIBLINGS (Art. 994)

In default of parents, the surviving spouse gets 100% alone, or splits 1/2 to Spouse and 1/2 to Illegitimate Siblings/Nephews/Nieces.

4. LEGITIMATE SIBLINGS BARRED (Art. 992)

The "brothers, sisters, nephews, nieces" in Art. 994 refer exclusively to illegitimate collaterals. Legitimate half-siblings are barred by the Iron Curtain.

PART III — Surviving Spouse (Arts. 995–1002)

9. Surviving Spouse Alone & With Legitimate Children (Arts. 995–996; Santillon v. Miranda)

Imagine a husband who dies intestate survived only by his wife and one legitimate son. The son argues that under Article 892 (on legitimes), when there is only one legitimate child, the spouse gets only 1/4 and the child gets 3/4.

In the absence of descendants, ascendants, and illegitimate children, the surviving spouse inherits the entire estate (without prejudice to concurring siblings/nephews/nieces under Art. 1001), with no reduction even if the marriage was celebrated in articulo mortis (Art. 995). When concurring with legitimate children (Art. 996), the spouse gets the same share as that of each legitimate child—and under the landmark doctrine in Santillon v. Miranda (14 SCRA 563), when there is only ONE legitimate child, they divide the estate 50% to the child and 50% to the surviving spouse because the statutory plural "children" includes the singular "child".

🪤 The Favorite Examination Trap — 1 Legitimate Child + Surviving Spouse (Testamentary vs. Intestate):
• In Testamentary Succession (Legitime Only — Art. 892): 1 Legitimate Child gets 1/2 as legitime; Surviving Spouse gets 1/4 as legitime (leaving 1/4 free disposal).
• In Intestate Succession (Whole Estate — Art. 996, Santillon v. Miranda): 1 Legitimate Child gets 1/2; Surviving Spouse gets 1/2!
• Marriage In Articulo Mortis (Art. 900 vs. Art. 995): If the decedent died within 3 months of a marriage in articulo mortis (without prior 5-year cohabitation), the spouse's legitime as sole heir drops from 1/2 to 1/3 under Art. 900—but in intestate succession (Art. 995), there is NO reduction (the spouse still takes 100%)!

10. Concurrence With Ascendants, Illegitimate Children & Mixed Groups (Arts. 997–1000)

Imagine a widow trying to calculate her share when she concurs with her in-laws (legitimate parents), or with illegitimate children, or with both at the same time.

When the surviving spouse concurs with legitimate ascendants alone (Art. 997) or with illegitimate children alone (Art. 998), the division is always a clean 1/2 – 1/2 split. When concurring with both legitimate ascendants and illegitimate children (Art. 1000), the shares are fixed at 1/2 to ascendants, 1/4 to the spouse, and 1/4 to illegitimate children. When concurring with legitimate children and illegitimate children (Art. 999), apply the 2 : 2 : 1 ratio unless the 1/2 legitime of the legitimate children is impaired.

11. Concurrence With Siblings/Nephews & Legal Separation Disqualification (Arts. 1001–1002)

Imagine an estranged husband and wife who have lived apart for 15 years without any court decree. When the husband dies leaving only his wife and two sisters, the sisters claim the wife is disqualified for abandoning him.

Under Article 1001, when the surviving spouse concurs with brothers, sisters, nephews, or nieces, the estate is divided 1/2 to the surviving spouse and 1/2 to the sibling/nephew/niece group (while the spouse excludes all more distant collaterals like uncles and cousins). Under Article 1002, a surviving spouse loses intestate rights ONLY if there is a final judicial decree of legal separation declaring that spouse the guilty party (mere de facto separation does not disqualify).

Symmetric Contrast Aphorisms on the Surviving Spouse (Arts. 995, 1001, 1002)
VALID MARRIAGE = HEIR
VOID / COMMON-LAW = ZERO		 A common-law partner or spouse in a void/bigamous marriage has zero intestate successional rights.
SIBLINGS/NEPHEWS = CONCUR (1/2)
UNCLES/COUSINS = EXCLUDED (0)		 The spouse shares 50-50 with brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces (Art. 1001), but completely excludes 3rd, 4th, and 5th-degree collaterals (Art. 995).
GUILTY IN DECREE = DISQUALIFIED
DE FACTO SEPARATION = STILL HEIR		 Without a final decree of legal separation finding the surviving spouse guilty (Art. 1002), mere physical separation does not strip intestate rights.

PART IV — Collateral Relatives & The State (Arts. 1003–1014)

12. First Collateral Group: Siblings, Nephews & Nieces (Arts. 1003–1008)

Imagine a bachelor who dies intestate without descendants, ascendants, illegitimate children, or a spouse. He is survived by a full-blood brother, a half-blood sister on his father's side, two nephews from a predeceased full-blood brother, and a grandnephew from another predeceased sister.

In default of primary and concurring heirs, collateral relatives inherit (Art. 1003), led by the First Collateral Group (brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces). Full-blood siblings take double the share (2:1) of half-blood siblings (Arts. 1004, 1006), while half-blood siblings alone divide equally regardless of paternal or maternal side (Art. 1007). Representation in the collateral line extends ONLY to nephews and nieces when concurring with surviving uncles/aunts (Arts. 1005, 1008; Abellana de Bacayo v. Ferraris de Borromeo)—never to grandnephews/grandnieces!

🪤 The Favorite Examination Trap — Abellana de Bacayo v. Ferraris de Borromeo (14 SCRA 986):
Suppose the decedent is survived by an Aunt (3rd degree) and the Children of a Predeceased First Cousin (5th degree), OR a Surviving Sister (2nd degree) and a Grandnephew (4th degree, child of a predeceased nephew).
• Rule: Representation in the collateral line stops strictly at children of brothers and sisters (nephews and nieces, 3rd degree). A grandnephew (4th degree) cannot represent a nephew, and an aunt (3rd degree) does NOT exclude a nephew/niece! Why? Because nephews and nieces (First Collateral Group, Arts. 1004–1008) ALWAYS exclude uncles and aunts (Second Collateral Group, Art. 1009) even though both are in the 3rd civil degree!

13. Second Collateral Group & The 5th-Degree Cap (Arts. 1009–1010)

Imagine a decedent with no siblings, nephews, or nieces. His surviving relatives are one maternal aunt (3rd degree) and three paternal first cousins (4th degree), plus a full-blood uncle and a half-blood uncle.

Only when there are neither brothers/sisters nor nephews/nieces do other collateral relatives inherit (Art. 1009), strictly observing the Rule of Proximity (Bagunu v. Piedad, 347 SCRA 71) up to the 5th civil degree limit (Art. 1010), and without any distinction between full-blood and half-blood (equal division within the same degree).

Collateral Degree Ladder & Rules (Arts. 1009–1010; Bagunu v. Piedad)
Civil Degree Relatives Included Priority & Blood Rule
2nd Degree Brothers and Sisters 1st Collateral Group; 2:1 Full vs. Half-blood applies
3rd Degree (Group 1) Nephews and Nieces (children of siblings) 1st Collateral Group; excludes Uncles/Aunts! 2:1 Full vs. Half-blood applies
3rd Degree (Group 2) Uncles and Aunts (siblings of parents) 2nd Collateral Group; excludes 4th degree; Full & Half-blood share EQUALLY (1:1)
4th Degree First Cousins, Grandnephews/Grandnieces, Great-Uncles/Great-Aunts 2nd Collateral Group; excludes 5th degree; Full & Half-blood share EQUALLY (1:1)
5th Degree (MAX) First Cousins Once Removed (child of 1st cousin), Second Cousins? (No—2nd cousins are 6th degree!) Statutory Cutoff (Art. 1010). 6th degree (e.g., second cousins) get ZERO.

14. The State & Escheat Proceedings (Arts. 1011–1014)

Imagine an elderly recluse who dies intestate without any known spouse or blood relative within the 5th degree. Four years after his properties are turned over to the city for public schools, a long-lost nephew surfaces with his birth certificate.

In default of all intestate heirs, the State inherits the whole estate (Art. 1011) as ultimate heir (bona vacantia) through judicial escheat proceedings under Rule 91 of the Rules of Court (Art. 1012), assigning personal property to the city/municipality of last residence and real property to where it is situated for the benefit of public schools and charitable institutions (Art. 1013), subject to a 5-year prescriptive period for any rightful heir to reclaim the property or its unspent proceeds (Art. 1014).

Memory aid: ESCHEAT (Arts. 1011–1014 & Rule 91) — R – P – R – 5
R Rule 91 Procedure (Art. 1012) Not automatic! Requires an escheat petition filed by the Solicitor General, court hearing, and publication once a week for 6 consecutive weeks.
P Personal Property (Art. 1013) Assigned to the municipality or city where the deceased last resided in the Philippines.
R Real Property & Non-Residents (Art. 1013) Assigned to the municipalities or cities where the property is situated (also applies to the whole estate if the deceased never resided in the Philippines), for public schools and charitable institutions.
5 5-Year Reclaim Window (Art. 1014) A rightful heir must appear and file a claim with the court within 5 years from the date the property was delivered to the State to recover the property (or unspent proceeds if sold); otherwise forever barred.

Top 5 Rules to Prioritize for Examination

  1. The 3 Exceptions to Equal Division in the Same Degree (L – B – R): Division by Paternal/Maternal Lines (Art. 987), Full/Half-Blood 2:1 ratio among siblings/nephews (Art. 1006), and Representation per stirpes (Art. 981/1005).
  2. Grandchildren Alone (Art. 982) vs. Nephews/Nieces Alone (Art. 975/1005): When all children are dead, grandchildren still inherit per stirpes; when all brothers/sisters are dead, nephews and nieces inherit per capita.
  3. The Legitime Impairment Guardrail (Arts. 983 & 999): Always test the 2 : 1 or 2 : 2 : 1 ratio first; if the legitimate children's collective share drops below 1/2 of the net estate, lock the legitimate children at 1/2 (and the spouse at her legitime), then divide the remainder among the illegitimate children.
  4. Article 992 Nuances (Classroom vs. Bar): Remember that Art. 992 applies only to intestate/legal succession (not wills), does not bar illegitimate siblings from inheriting from each other (Delgado), and was modified in Aquino v. Aquino (2021) to allow grandchildren to represent their parents regardless of legitimacy.
  5. First vs. Second Collateral Group (Arts. 1005 & 1009): Although both Nephews/Nieces and Uncles/Aunts are 3rd-degree relatives, Nephews/Nieces belong to the First Collateral Group and completely exclude Uncles/Aunts (Bacayo v. Borromeo), and the 2:1 full/half-blood rule disappears once you reach Uncles/Aunts and Cousins (Art. 1009).
