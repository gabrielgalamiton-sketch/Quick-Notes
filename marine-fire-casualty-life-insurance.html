  • Insurance Commission Adjudicatory Jurisdiction (Sec. 439): The jurisdictional cap for claims filed before the Insurance Commissioner was dramatically raised from ₱100,000.00 under the old law to ₱5,000,000.00 (concurrent with the RTC). For claims exceeding ₱5,000,000.00, the RTC retains exclusive original jurisdiction. The doctrine of exhaustion of administrative remedies does not apply.
  • CMVLI No-Fault Maximum Limit (Sec. 391 & IC Circulars): The statutory No-Fault indemnity cap has been increased to ₱30,000.00 per person for all motor vehicles (applicable strictly to death or bodily injury; never property damage).
  • CMVLI Third-Party Liability Limits: Updated schedule caps third-party liability at ₱200,000.00 per vehicle (with death/burial indemnity at ₱200,000.00, plus up to ₱10,000.00 for unscheduled incidental expenses).
  • Forfeited Beneficiary Proceeds Distribution (Sec. 12, as amended): When a beneficiary willfully kills the insured, the proceeds pass strictly in order: (1) to other qualified beneficiaries; (2) under the policy contract; (3) to the insured's estate.
  • Microinsurance Quantitative Ceilings (Sec. 187): Daily contributions/premiums cannot exceed 7.5% of the Metro Manila non-agricultural daily minimum wage; maximum guaranteed benefits cannot exceed 1,000 times said daily minimum wage.

PART I — MARINE INSURANCE: RISK, CONCEALMENT, REPRESENTATION, AND IMPLIED WARRANTIES

1. Fundamentals & Scope of Marine Insurance

Imagine a bulk carrier loaded with 50,000 bags of Philippine sugar departing Iloilo for San Francisco. Halfway across the Pacific, rogue waves crack the deck hatches and inundate the hold, while a parallel shipment on land is destroyed when a cargo trailer collides with a concrete barrier on the North Luzon Expressway. Both shippers claim under "marine insurance"—yet one traverses the high seas while the other never left dry asphalt.

Under Section 101 of R.A. No. 10607, marine insurance is broadly defined as transportation insurance covering loss or damage to vessels, craft, aircraft, vehicles, goods, freights, cargoes, and all other property or interests exposed to any and all risks of navigation, transit, or transportation.

TRANSPORTATION INSURANCE = OCEAN MARINE (Navigable Waters) + INLAND MARINE (Land/Transit)
Classification Physical Locus & Subject Matter Core Coverage Highlights
Ocean Marine Insurance High seas, territorial seas, and navigable waterways. Covers four distinct maritime interests: Hull (vessel structure), Cargo (merchandise carried), Freightage (expected earnings or charter hire), and Liability (collision damage to third parties).
Inland Marine Insurance Land, highways, railways, air transit, and fixed navigation aids. Covers goods in domestic over-the-land transit (motor trucks, rail freight), air cargo, and instrumentalities of transportation (bridges, tunnels, docks, piers, and dry docks).

Perils of the Sea vs. Perils of the Ship

In standard ocean marine insurance, the insurer undertakes to indemnify the insured strictly against perils of the sea, and never perils of the ship, unless the policy is expressly drafted as an "All-Risks" policy.

Basis of Comparison Perils of the Sea Perils of the Ship
Nature of Risk Extraordinary, fortuitous, and violent casualties of the elements (tempests, typhoons, lightning, collision with icebergs/rocks, piracy). Ordinary, natural, and inevitable action of wind and waves, natural decay, inherent vice, or normal wear and tear of the ship.
Proximate Cause External, accidental, and unexpected maritime hazards that could not be prevented by ordinary human skill and prudence. Internal structural defects, mechanical breakdown, defective equipment, improper cargo stowage, or crew negligence without an intervening fortuitous event.
Standard Policy Coverage COVERED as a general rule under an ordinary marine insurance policy. NOT COVERED; the shipowner or insured bears the entire loss.
"All-Risks" Policy Coverage Covered automatically unless expressly excluded. Covered by exception, provided the loss is fortuitous and not caused by the insured's willful misconduct or fraud.
Memory aid: SEA = OUTSIDE & EXTRAORDINARY | SHIP = INSIDE & ORDINARY
"The sea did not sink the ship; the ship's rotten timbers let the sea in."
If ordinary sea swells sink a vessel because of rusted plates, it is a Peril of the Ship. If an extraordinary storm tears off sound steel plates, it is a Peril of the Sea.

Vessel in Navigation vs. Unfinished Vessel: Marine vs. Fire Insurance

A vessel damaged by fire can be governed by either Fire Insurance or Marine Insurance, depending strictly on the operational status of the craft at the time of contracting:

UNFINISHED VESSEL IN SHARD / DRYDOCK Still under construction, never been afloat for a voyage, or raw materials in a dockyard → GOVERNED BY FIRE INSURANCE
VS
VESSEL ENGAGED IN NAVIGATION Afloat, commissioned, or ready for maritime service (even if docked temporarily or insured against fire risk only) → GOVERNED BY MARINE INSURANCE
⚠️ THE FAVORITE EXAMINATION TRAP (Marine All-Risks vs. Perils of the Ship):

Bar examiners love asking whether an "All-Risks" marine policy covers a loss resulting from a peril of the ship. Under settled jurisprudence, an "All-Risks" policy covers all losses occurring during the voyage—even if arising from a peril of the ship—except when the loss is occasioned by the willful misconduct, fraud, or intentional destruction of the insured. Once the insured proves a fortuitous physical loss during the voyage, the burden shifts entirely to the insurer to prove that the cause falls cleanly within an express policy exclusion!

2. Concealment and Representation in Marine Insurance

Imagine a shipowner in Cebu applying for hull insurance on a vessel berthed in Davao. Harbor workers and seamen on an adjacent vessel notice that the ship is tilting heavily to its port side due to uneven cargo stowage. The shipowner in Cebu has not inspected the ship personally, but receiving word of this deck chatter, wonders: "Do I have to tell the insurer about random gossip from neighboring sailors?"

While Section 26 defines concealment generally as the neglect to communicate that which a party knows and ought to communicate, Section 109 elevates marine insurance to a much higher standard of uberrimae fidei (utmost good faith): each party must communicate all information possessed material to the risk and state the exact and whole truth.

ORDINARY CONCEALMENT = Material facts known | MARINE CONCEALMENT = EXACT & WHOLE TRUTH + ALL INFO POSSESSED

Disclosure of Information, Belief, or Expectation of Third Persons (Sec. 110)

Under Section 35 (ordinary insurance), opinions or judgment need not be disclosed. But Section 110 establishes a strict statutory exception for marine policies: information of the belief or expectation of a third person, in reference to a material fact, is material and must be communicated.

👨‍🏫 Professor's Classroom Illustration — The Listing Vessel:
While Vessel X is docked at port being loaded with cargo, seamen and deck officers from an adjacent vessel observe the operations and remark that Vessel X is listing heavily to one side ("lumaladlad o bumabanking ang barko") because the cargo is not properly stowed (not properly laden). Even though these observers are third persons completely unconnected to the shipowner, crew, or cargo owner, their belief is formed by maritime industry experts. Under Section 110, the insured must disclose this third-party expectation to the insurer!

Qualified Concealment: When Concealment Does Not Vitiate the Entire Policy (Sec. 112)

In non-marine insurance, any material concealment voids the contract entirely. In contrast, Section 112 creates a unique rule of Qualified Concealment: concealment of any of five specific matters does not vitiate the entire contract, but merely exonerates the insurer from a loss resulting directly from the risk concealed.

Memory aid: N – A – S – W – F (Qualified Marine Concealment)
Concealing these 5 matters does NOT void the policy unless the loss results directly from that concealed risk.
NNational CharacterThe nationality of the insured or carrying vessel.
AArrest / CaptureThe liability of the thing insured to capture and detention.
SSeizure / Foreign TradeLiability to seizure from breach of foreign laws of trade.
WWant of DocumentsThe want of necessary shipping or customs documents.
FFalse PapersThe use of false, simulated, or fraudulent ship papers.
SEC. 112 CAUSAL TEST: CONCEALED FACT == ACTUAL CAUSE OF LOSS → EXONERATION | INDEPENDENT PERIL → INSURER MUST PAY!
👨‍🏫 Professor's Classroom Illustration — The Contraband vs. The Typhoon:
A shipowner conceals that his vessel carries contraband that exposes it to maritime pirates (concealing liability to capture and detention). During the voyage, no pirates attack the vessel. Instead, the ship sinks during a violent typhoon (peril of the sea) or is impounded because of an unexpected lack of port health clearances (want of necessary documents). Because the concealed risk (piracy) did not cause the loss ("hindi nagtama ang rason at ang fact concealed"), the contract remains valid and the insurer remains liable!

Marine Concealment vs. Ordinary Concealment

Feature / Legal Rule Ordinary (Non-Marine) Insurance Marine Insurance
Duty of Disclosure Communicate material facts known or that ought to be known (Secs. 26–28). Strictest uberrimae fidei: state the exact and whole truth and disclose all information possessed (Sec. 109).
Third-Person Beliefs / Opinions Not required; opinion and judgment are immaterial even if inquired into (Sec. 35). Mandatory; information of belief or expectation of third persons regarding a material fact must be disclosed (Sec. 110).
Causal Connection to Loss Never required; concealment rescinds the policy even if loss is wholly unrelated. Required for the 5 matters under Sec. 112; exoneration occurs only if the loss results from the specific risk concealed.
Representations (Secs. 113–115) Material misrepresentation entitles rescission (Sec. 45). Intentional falsity allows rescission of entire contract (Sec. 113); eventual falsity of expectation does not avoid policy absent fraud (Sec. 114).

3. Implied Warranties in Marine Insurance (Seaworthiness and Deviation)

Imagine an exporter booking 1,000 crates of electronics on a commercial cargo ship. The marine policy issued to the cargo owner makes no mention of the ship's engine condition, crew certifications, or voyage route. When the ship sinks on a calm, sunny day because the captain was unlicensed and the vessel was overloaded, the insurer denies the cargo claim. The cargo owner protests: "I don't own the ship! Why am I penalized for the carrier's secret flaws?"

In ordinary insurance, warranties must be express (Sec. 70). In Marine Insurance, however, the law conclusively reads Implied Warranties into every contract by operation of law, even if the policy is completely silent:

The Implied Warranty of Seaworthiness (Secs. 115–121)

Under Section 116, a ship is seaworthy when it is reasonably fit to perform the service and to encounter the ordinary perils of the voyage contemplated by the parties. Seaworthiness is a relative term—a vessel fit for calm river transport may be hopelessly unseaworthy for the open Pacific Ocean.

Memory aid: L – M – C – E (Scope of Seaworthiness under Sec. 119)
LLaden ProperlyProperly stowed, balanced, not listing, and not overloaded past Plimsoll line.
MMaster CompetentProvided with a licensed, skilled, and sober ship captain.
CCrew SufficientManned by a sufficient number of competent, certified officers and seamen.
EEquipment & StoresFurnished with anchors, radar, charts, fuel, fresh water, and seaworthy for cargo type (Sec. 121).

When Seaworthiness Must Exist: General Rule vs. 3 Statutory Exceptions

Under Section 117, the General Rule is that seaworthiness must exist strictly at the time of the commencement of the risk. Prior unseaworthiness cured before departure, or subsequent unseaworthiness arising during the voyage, does not breach the warranty (provided repairs are not unreasonably delayed under Sec. 120).

Type of Marine Policy Statutory Timing of Seaworthiness (Secs. 117–118) Analytical Rule
1. Time Policy Must be seaworthy at the commencement of every voyage undertaken during the insured period (Sec. 117[a]). In a 1-year time policy, the risk recommences each time a new voyage begins.
2. Cargo / Transshipment Policy Each vessel upon which cargo is shipped or transshipped must be seaworthy at the commencement of each particular voyage (Sec. 117[b]). Applies to multi-leg container shipments (e.g., Cebu to Manila, transshipped Manila to Tokyo); each feeder and mother ship must be seaworthy upon departure.
3. Staged Voyage Policy Ship must be seaworthy for each specific portion at the commencement of that portion (Sec. 118). A voyage involving river transit followed by ocean crossing requires fitness for river at port of origin, and ocean fitness before clearing the estuary.

Cargo Owner's Obligation of Seaworthiness (*Roque* & *Delsan* Doctrines)

⚠️ THE CARGO OWNER'S EXAMINATION TRAP:

Under Section 115 and settled Supreme Court jurisprudence (Roque v. IAC, G.R. No. L-66935; Delsan Transport Lines v. CA, G.R. No. 127897), the implied warranty of seaworthiness attaches even when the policy is procured by the CARGO OWNER! The cargo shipper cannot excuse unseaworthiness by arguing that he does not own or control the vessel. The shipper has absolute freedom in selecting the common carrier; if the vessel is unseaworthy, the cargo insurer is completely discharged from liability. The cargo owner's sole remedy is to sue the common carrier for breach of the contract of carriage!

The Implied Warranty Against Improper Deviation (Secs. 123–128)

A vessel must proceed along the course fixed by mercantile usage, or the most natural and direct route. Under Section 125, deviation takes four forms: (1) departure from the course; (2) unreasonable delay; (3) commencement of an entirely different voyage; or (4) departure from specified navigation limits.

Proper Deviation (Sec. 126) — Policy Retained Improper Deviation (Secs. 127–128) — Insurer Exonerated
1. Uncontrollable circumstances (violent typhoons, currents, icebergs).
2. Comply with warranty or avoid a peril (insured or not).
3. Good faith upon reasonable grounds to avoid a peril.
4. Saving human life or relieving another vessel in distress. 		Every deviation not specified in Section 126 is improper.
Legal Effect (Sec. 128): Exonerates the insurer from any loss happening subsequent to the improper deviation, whether or not the deviation increased the risk or caused the loss!
👨‍🏫 Professor's Classroom Illustrations — Deviation Scenarios:
  • The Captain's Dream (Bar Problem): A captain dreams of his deceased grandfather warning him to change course. He alters route based solely on the dream and hits a reef. Ruling: Improper deviation. Section 126(c) requires good faith based on reasonable grounds of belief (meteorological data, radar), not dreams or superstition. Insurer is exonerated.
  • Wisdom Tooth Medical Emergency: A third officer suffers an acute, debilitating tooth infection, sleepless for days with fever and no doctor aboard. Captain deviates 2 days to Malaysia for emergency surgery. Ruling: Proper deviation under Section 126(d) (saving human life and health in extreme distress). Policy remains valid.

PART II — MARINE LOSSES, ABANDONMENT, AVERAGES, FIRE INSURANCE, AND CASUALTY INSURANCE

4. Classification of Marine Losses, Constructive Total Loss, and Abandonment

Imagine a ₱100 Million luxury yacht driven onto a jagged coral reef off Tubbataha. The hull is severely cracked, but the ship has not sunk. Marine salvors inform the owner: "We can tow and patch the hull, but the specialized salvage flotilla and drydock rebuild will cost ₱82 Million." The owner wonders: "Must I spend ₱82M to fix a ₱100M yacht, or can I hand the wreck to the insurer and demand my full ₱100M?"

Under Sections 130–132, marine losses are classified into partial loss or total loss. A total loss is either an Actual Total Loss or a Constructive Total Loss.

BAR EXAM VOCABULARY RULE: Always write "Actual Total Loss" or "Constructive Total Loss" — NEVER merely "actual" or "constructive" loss!
Category Statutory Definition & Causes (Sec. 133 & Sec. 141) Right of Abandonment
Actual Total Loss (Sec. 133) 1. Total physical destruction of the thing.
2. Irretrievable loss by sinking into unrecoverable depths or breaking up.
3. Damage rendering the thing completely valueless for its intended purpose.
4. Permanent deprivation of possession at port of destination (seizure). 		NEITHER POSSIBLE NOR NECESSARY (Sec. 137). There is nothing left to abandon; insured claims full indemnity outright.
Constructive Total Loss (Sec. 134, 141) Property is not completely destroyed, but damage or cost of recovery exceeds three-fourths (3/4 or 75%) of its value.
• Ship: voyage cannot be performed without > 3/4 expense.
• Cargo: forwarding expenses exceed > 3/4 cargo value. 		EXCLUSIVELY APPLICABLE. Insured has the legal right to abandon and claim for a total loss.

The Two Options of the Insured in a Constructive Total Loss

OPTION 1: ABANDON AND CLAIM TOTAL INDEMNITY Relinquish all title and salvage to the insurer → Recover 100% of insured policy value (Sec. 140).
OR
OPTION 2: RETAIN SALVAGE AND CLAIM ACTUAL PARTIAL LOSS Keep ownership of damaged wreck → Recover only the actual assessed cost of partial damage.

Statutory Requisites of a Valid Abandonment (Secs. 142–147)

Memory aid: T – U – R – F – N – C (Requisites for Abandonment)
TThree-Fourths (3/4)Damage or cost of recovery must exceed 75% of the value (Sec. 141).
UUnconditionalMust be total and absolute; neither partial nor conditional (Sec. 142).
RReasonable TimeMade within reasonable time after receiving reliable info of loss (Sec. 143).
FFactualInformation must be true; invalid if vessel restored before notice (Sec. 144).
NNotice GivenExplicit notice; if oral, written notice must follow within 7 days (Sec. 145).
CCause SpecifiedMust state the particular cause showing constructive total loss (Secs. 146–147).
INSURER REFUSAL (Sec. 151): Acceptance NOT required! If requisites are met, insurer is liable as upon an actual total loss.

5. Marine Averages, Measure of Indemnity & Automatic Co-Insurance

Imagine a storm rocking a freighter in the Babuyan Channel. The captain orders deckhands to throw 100 shipping containers into the sea to lighten the vessel and prevent it from capsizing. Across the cargo hold, another single crate was crushed when an unsecured forklift rolled into it during transit. Both suffered cargo loss—yet one shipper gets reimbursed by everyone on board, while the other stands alone.

Under Section 138 and Articles 806–818 of the Code of Commerce, an Average is any extraordinary or accidental expense incurred during the voyage for the preservation of the vessel, cargo, or both, and all damages suffered by the ship or cargo.

Basis of Comparison Particular (Simple) Average General (Gross) Average
Definition & Nature Damages or expenses caused to vessel or cargo which have not inured to the common benefit (Art. 809). Damages deliberately caused in order to save the vessel and cargo from a real, known peril, inuring to the common benefit (Art. 811).
Essential Requisites 1. Accidental or isolated damage;
2. Absence of common danger or deliberate sacrifice;
3. Benefits only one interest.		 1. Common danger to ship and cargo;
2. Deliberate sacrifice of part;
3. Successful saving of the adventure;
4. Legal formalities observed by master.
Who Bears the Loss Borne alone by the owner of the damaged thing or expense (Art. 810). Borne proportionately by all benefited interests (shipowner and saved cargo owners) by contribution (Art. 812).
Insurer's Liability Insurer of damaged item pays, unless policy contains a "Free From Particular Average" (FPA) clause. Marine insurers of saved vessel and cargo are liable to pay the general average contributions (Sec. 166).
👨‍🏫 Professor's Classroom Illustration — "The Boat is Sinking" (Jettison):
To prevent a sinking vessel from capsizing, the captain deliberately jettisons Cargo A belonging to Shipper X. The vessel and the remaining cargoes of Shippers Y and Z are successfully saved. Because the sacrifice inured to the common benefit of all, it is a General Average. The shipowner, Shipper Y, and Shipper Z (and their respective marine insurers) must contribute ratably to reimburse Shipper X! In contrast, if deckhands brawling on deck accidentally kick a crate overboard, that is an isolated Particular Average borne solely by that crate's owner.

Automatic Co-Insurance in Marine Insurance (Sec. 159)

Under Section 159, in every partial loss under a marine policy, when property is insured for less than its total value, the insured is automatically a co-insurer for the difference by operation of law:

$$\text{Insurer's Liability} = \frac{\text{Amount of Insurance}}{\text{Total Value of Property}} \times \text{Amount of Partial Loss}$$

Scenario A: Full Insurance Coverage (₱10M Value / ₱10M Policy)
Ship suffers a ₱2,000,000 partial collision damage.

Insurer covers 100% of the partial loss because the property was insured for its full value.

Scenario B: Under-Insurance (₱10M Value / ₱5M Policy)
Ship suffers the same ₱2,000,000 partial collision damage.

By law (Sec. 159), (₱5M / ₱10M) × ₱2M = ₱1,000,000. The owner is an automatic co-insurer for the remaining ₱1,000,000!
"In Marine Insurance, co-insurance is an automatic law; in Fire Insurance, co-insurance exists only if written in black and white."

6. Fire Insurance and Allied Risks

Imagine a dry goods merchant whose building is insured against fire. During cooking in the ground-floor pantry, an LPG stove emits thick soot and blackens the newly painted ceilings. Later that week, a lit candle falls onto a nylon curtain, turning the living quarters into an inferno. The merchant files claims for both events.

Under Section 169, fire insurance covers direct physical loss caused by fire, plus allied perils (lightning, windstorm, earthquake) by rider. To recover, the loss must be proximately caused by a Hostile Fire, not a Friendly Fire:

Basis of Comparison Friendly Fire Hostile Fire
Location & Confinement Burns in a place where it was intended to burn and ought to be (inside stove, fireplace, furnace, or candle wick). Escapes from its intended confinement, or originates where no fire was intended to be.
Insurer Liability NOT LIABLE. Damage caused purely by heat, smoke, or soot from a confined flame is not recoverable. FULLY LIABLE. Covers all direct fire, smoke, water, and firefighting damage once the flame escapes.
👨‍🏫 Professor's Classroom Illustration — Stoves, Curtains & Wildfires:
A gas stove flame emitting smoke that stains walls is a Friendly Fire; the insurer pays nothing. But if the flame jumps from the burner onto a dish towel and ignites the kitchen, it transforms into a Hostile Fire, making the insurer fully liable. Similarly, an uncontrolled forest wildfire that spreads onto insured residential grounds operates as a Hostile Fire.

Alteration of Insured Property (Secs. 170–172)

Memory aid: U – L – W – C – I (Requisites for Fire Rescission via Alteration)
UUse AlteredUse or condition of the insured property is physically changed.
LLimited by PolicyThe use or condition is expressly or impliedly limited by the contract.
WWithout ConsentAlteration is effected without the insurer's written consent.
CControl of InsuredMade by means within the insured's control (includes tenants/staff).
IIncreases the RiskMaterially increases the risk of fire (Sec. 170) or violates express terms (Sec. 172).
👨‍🏫 Professor's Classroom Illustration — "Chichirya" to Fireworks:
A warehouse owner insures his facility declaring that it will store only non-hazardous snacks (chichirya) and frozen food. During the "ber" months leading to New Year, he leases the floor space to store fireworks (paputok) without notifying the insurer. Because this alteration was within his control and drastically increased the fire risk, the insurer is entitled to rescind the policy under Section 170!

Special Clauses in Fire Policies

  • Option to Rebuild Clause (Sec. 174): In open policies, the insurer may reserve the option to rebuild or repair the destroyed property instead of paying cash indemnity.
  • Fallen Building Clause: If the building collapses from a non-fire cause (earthquake, decay, structural fault), all fire insurance immediately ceases. A subsequent fire breaking out among the ruins is not covered!

7. Casualty and Third-Party Liability (TPL) Insurance

Imagine an armored delivery truck colliding with a motorcycle along EDSA, severely injuring the rider and causing ₱3,000,000 in medical and moral damages. The delivery company has a Third-Party Liability (TPL) policy with a coverage limit of ₱500,000. In court, the injured motorcyclist asks the judge to hold the insurance company solidarily liable for the full ₱3,000,000 award.

Under Section 176, casualty insurance covers loss or liability arising from accident or mishap. In Third-Party Liability (TPL) insurance, the insurable interest lies in the safety of other persons and their property who might sue the insured for damages.

NO SOLIDARY LIABILITY: Tortfeasor liability = UNLIMITED (Tort) | Insurer liability = LIMITED TO POLICY FACE VALUE (Contract)

Direct Action by Injured Third Parties: Liability vs. Actual Loss Policies

Type of Liability Policy When Insurer's Obligation Attaches Can the Injured Third Party Sue Insurer Directly?
Indemnity Against Liability Attaches the moment the insured becomes legally liable upon the occurrence of the accident (*Guingona v. Del Monte*). YES. Injured third party can sue insurer directly or implead it with the insured.
Indemnity Against Actual Loss / Payment Attaches only after the insured has actually paid the judgment claim in cash to the victim. NO. Third party has no direct right of action against insurer; he must execute against insured first.
⚠️ THE "NO ACTION" CLAUSE TRAP:

A "No Action" clause states that no suit will lie against the insurer until a final judgment is first obtained against the insured. The Supreme Court has ruled that where the policy is clearly one of indemnity against liability, a "No Action" clause cannot prevail over the Rules of Court permitting third-party complaints! The insurer may be impleaded directly to avoid multiplicity of suits (Shafer v. Judge, RTC Olongapo, G.R. No. 78848).

PART III — LIFE INSURANCE, SURETYSHIP, CMVLI, CLAIMS SETTLEMENT, AND THE INSURANCE COMMISSION

8. Life Insurance: Classifications, Assignment, and Causes of Death

Imagine a policyholder who designates his sister as beneficiary. Ten years later, in financial distress, he assigns the life insurance policy to a casual business creditor who has no personal relationship with him. The insurer argues: "The assignee has no insurable interest in your life!" Meanwhile, the insured is diagnosed with severe clinical depression and tragically takes his own life during a psychotic episode.

Under Sections 181–182, life insurance is insurance on human lives payable upon death or surviving a specified period. Life insurance differs from accident insurance: life policies build cash values and pay upon natural or accidental death, whereas accident insurance covers accidental injury, dismemberment, and death during lifetime.

Classification Key Operational Mechanics & Payment Triggers
1. Ordinary / Whole Life Premiums payable for life; proceeds payable strictly upon death of the insured.
2. Limited Payment Life Premiums paid for a limited term (e.g., 10 or 20 years); policy remains fully paid up for life thereafter.
3. Endowment Policy If insured dies within period, beneficiaries recover face value; if insured survives the period, face value is paid to the insured himself while alive.
4. Term Insurance Pure temporary risk protection for a fixed term; if insured outlives the term, no benefit is payable.
5. Variable Life (VUL) Combines life protection with an investment component linked to equity/bond funds (Secs. 238–240).
6. Industrial Life (Sec. 235) Premiums payable weekly or monthly, face amount capped at statutory limit (₱2,000 under baseline Code), with words "Industrial Policy" printed on face.

Transfer and Assignment of Life Policies (Secs. 184–185)

SEC. 184: NO INSURABLE INTEREST REQUIRED IN ASSIGNEE | SEC. 185: NOTICE TO INSURER NOT REQUIRED UNLESS EXPRESS

Unlike property policies, life insurance may pass by transfer, will, or succession to any person, whether he has an insurable interest or not (Sec. 184). Notice to the insurer is not necessary to preserve validity unless expressly required by the policy contract (Sec. 185).

Specific Causes of Death & Insurer Liability

Cause of Death Statutory Rule & Requisites (Secs. 12 & 183)
Legal Execution COVERED. Death by legal execution is an assumed risk; beneficiaries recover unless the beneficiary committed the capital crime.
Suicide (Sec. 183) Insurer is liable ONLY IF committed:
1. After 2 years from policy issue or last reinstatement;
2. After a shorter period stipulated in the policy; OR
3. In a state of insanity (payable REGARDLESS of time elapsed, even on Day 1!).
Willful Killing by Beneficiary (Sec. 12) General Rule: Beneficiary's interest is FORFEITED if principal, accomplice, or accessory.

10. Compulsory Motor Vehicle Liability Insurance (CMVLI) & The "No-Fault" Clause

Imagine a passenger riding in a public utility jeepney along Commonwealth Avenue. A speeding dump truck blows a tire, swerves across the median, and crushes the jeepney. The passenger suffers broken legs. The jeepney driver was 100% blameless, while the dump truck driver fled the scene. Who pays the passenger's hospital bills immediately without litigation?

Under Sections 386–402, CMVLI is mandatory for LTO registration to provide immediate financial assistance for death and bodily injury sustained in vehicular accidents. PUV operators—not drivers—must pay the premiums.

The "No-Fault" Indemnity Clause (Sec. 391 & IC Circulars)

NO-FAULT CEILING = ₱30,000.00 | STRICTLY DEATH / BODILY INJURY | NEVER PROPERTY DAMAGE!
Claimant Status Which Insurer Must Pay No-Fault Indemnity? Underlying Legal Doctrine
Passenger / Vehicle Occupant Claim lies against the insurer of the vehicle in which the claimant was riding (Sec. 391[c]). Even if that carrying vehicle was 100% blameless, its insurer must pay! The paying insurer can seek reimbursement from the responsible party later.
Pedestrian / Third Party (Not an Occupant) Claim lies against the insurer of the directly offending vehicle. Victim files directly against the striking vehicle's insurer without proving fault.
Routing Rule: Passengers claim from their own carrier's insurer; Pedestrians claim from the striking vehicle's insurer.

Authorized Driver Clause vs. Theft Clause (*Villacorta* & *Palermo*)

👨‍🏫 Professor's Classroom Illustration — Lending the Car to Friend B:
  • Scenario 1 (Valid License): Car owner A lends car to B. B holds a valid driver's license. Accident occurs. B is an authorized driver; claim is COVERED.
  • Scenario 2 (Expired License / Violated Restriction): B drives with A's permission but has an expired license, or drives at night violating a "daytime driving only" restriction. B is NOT an authorized driver; claim is DENIED.
  • Scenario 3 (Unauthorized Taking / Joyriding): B takes A's car without permission (or a repair shop mechanic joyrides). The car crashes. The insurer cannot deny liability under the Authorized Driver Clause; the owner recovers under the THEFT CLAUSE regardless of whether the driver had a license! (Villacorta v. IC; Palermo v. Pyramid Ins.).

Change of Ownership in CMVLI (Sec. 395)

A transfer of motor vehicle ownership does not suspend the policy, provided three requisites concur: (1) Insurer agrees to continue policy; (2) Endorsement made on policy; (3) Signed duplicate endorsement filed with the LTO. Selling via an "open deed of sale" without notifying the insurer and LTO suspends coverage!

⚠️ CRIMINAL FRAUDULENT CLAIMS (Sec. 251):

Presenting fraudulent claims or false documents in support of insurance claims is a criminal offense punishable by a fine not exceeding twice the amount claimed, imprisonment of two (2) years, or both, at court discretion!

Prescriptive Periods in Insurance Actions (Sec. 63 vs. Sec. 397)

  • General Insurance (Sec. 63): Policy may stipulate a period to sue, but it cannot be less than one (1) year from accrual of the cause of action. Cause of action accrues upon final rejection/denial of the claim by the insurer, never from the date of the casualty! If the policy has no stipulation or if the clause is void (< 1 year), the action prescribes in ten (10) years under Article 1144 of the Civil Code.
  • CMVLI Special Prescriptive Periods (Sec. 397): Written notice of claim must be filed within six (6) months from the accident; court or IC action must be commenced within one (1) year from denial.

Powers & Adjudicatory Jurisdiction of the Insurance Commission (Secs. 437–439)

Jurisdictional Tier Governing Provision Statutory Scope & Forum
CMVLI Claims Sec. 398 Exclusive Original Jurisdiction of the Insurance Commissioner.
Claims UP TO ₱5,000,000.00 Sec. 439 Concurrent Original Jurisdiction between the Insurance Commissioner and the Regional Trial Courts (RTC). Claimants may file directly in the RTC without exhausting administrative remedies!
Claims EXCEEDING ₱5,000,000.00 Sec. 439 & BP 129 Exclusive Original Jurisdiction of the RTC. The Insurance Commissioner has no adjudicatory authority.

Top Rules to Prioritize for Examination

  1. The Section 112 Causal Connection Rule: Unlike general insurance where any concealment vitiates the contract, concealing the 5 qualified matters (N-A-S-W-F) avoids liability ONLY if the casualty arose directly from that concealed risk.
  2. The Cargo Owner's Seaworthiness Trap: Shippers warrant carrier fitness. An unseaworthy vessel kills the cargo insurance claim; recourse lies against the carrier under maritime carriage contracts.
  3. CMVLI No-Fault Routing: Injured passengers claim against the carrying vehicle's insurer without proving fault; pedestrians claim against the striking vehicle. No-fault never pays property damage.
  4. The ₱5,000,000 Jurisdictional Divide: Insurance Commissioner and RTC share concurrent jurisdiction up to ₱5M. Claims above ₱5M belong strictly to the RTC. Exhaustion of administrative remedies is inapplicable.
  5. Accrual of Prescription: The clock starts on the date of final claim denial, never on the date of casualty. Clauses requiring suits within less than 1 year from denial are void.
